Warehouses and yards: docks, gates and plates

A dock camera does its best work mounted inside the bay looking out, high enough to read the trailer number and see the pallet cross the threshold. A plate reader at the gate is a separate camera with its own narrow angle and fast shutter; one wide camera cannot both show the whole yard and read a plate at night. Industrial block buildings in Maspeth usually mean conduit for every run, and long buildings run into the 328-foot limit on a single Cat6 run, so plan a second network switch or a fiber link to the far end. Budget for a lift on mounting day.

Storefronts on the avenues: the register, the gate and the sidewalk

Once the roll-down gate is closed, every camera inside is blind to the street. Put one vandal-resistant dome outside under the sign or awning to cover the gate and the entrance, aimed at your frontage rather than across the street into apartments. Inside, glass storefronts throw strong backlight at the door camera, so ask for wide dynamic range on that one and 8MP for faces. If your register system supports it, a text overlay ties each sale to the matching video, which settles most "I paid with a twenty" arguments in seconds.

Restaurants: grease, steam and no audio

Keep kitchen cameras out of the hood's path and in sealed housings, and put lens cleaning on the closing checklist, because a grease film turns a 4K picture into fog within weeks. Leave the microphones off: capturing other people's talk without being in the conversation can count as eavesdropping under Penal Law §250.05, and §250.45 keeps lenses out of restrooms and changing areas entirely.

The day someone asks for the footage

A claim letter often arrives weeks after the incident, and that is where systems fail: the clip has already been overwritten. Set retention to cover the longest delay you have seen, and check it with the security camera storage calculator before you buy drives. Then:

  1. Name two people who know how to export a clip, not just the owner.
  2. Keep the recorder clock synced to an internet time server so the timestamp holds up.
  3. Export in the recorder's original format, with its player, onto a USB drive, plus an MP4 copy for easy viewing.
  4. Write down the camera name, date and time range of every export and who it went to.
  5. Never hand over the recorder itself; give copies.
  6. If a lawyer's letter mentions a claim, lock the relevant footage so the recorder does not overwrite it.

Paperwork before the cameras go live

  • Staff notice. Tell employees in writing where cameras are and post the notice. New York's monitoring notice statute (Civil Rights Law §52-c) is written around phone, email and internet monitoring, but a clear camera notice prevents most disputes.
  • Face recognition. NYC's biometric identifier law (Local Law 3 of 2021) requires a sign at the entrance if you use it. Ordinary person detection is not face recognition.
  • Retail Worker Safety Act. Retail employers with 10 or more workers in New York must have a written workplace violence prevention policy and training. Cameras are not required by it, but good footage supports the policy.
  • Landlord and building. Get written permission for facade drilling and roof access, and have the installer's insurance certificate ready for management.
  • Historic districts. In Jackson Heights, Sunnyside Gardens and Douglaston, keep exterior mounts small and reversible, and check whether Landmarks review applies.

Frequently asked questions

How many cameras does a small Queens store need?

Most small shops start with 4 to 6: the door, the register, one or two over the aisles, the stockroom or back door, and one outside covering the gate. A fisheye can sometimes replace two aisle cameras.

Local recorder or cloud for a single Queens location?

With only one site, owning the recorder tends to cost less over a few years since nothing is billed per camera each month. A cloud plan makes more sense when one manager watches several locations.

Can I use business cameras to watch employees?

You can record work areas you have a legitimate reason to watch, with written notice to staff. Restrooms and changing rooms are off limits to any camera, and audio should stay off to avoid eavesdropping problems under New York law.

My landlord already has cameras in the building. Is that enough?

Those cameras and their footage belong to the landlord, and getting a clip depends on their schedule and retention. Most tenants put their own system inside their space and ask the landlord only for permission to mount and run cable.

Can an existing system from another installer be taken over?

Yes. Abandoned or half-finished systems get adopted, expanded or moved from analog to IP, and any camera or recorder that still works well stays in service.
