DVR Upgrade NYC: Test the Cameras You Already Own Before Buying Anything

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-09

DVR Upgrade NYC jobs usually begin with someone assuming the whole camera system is finished. More often only the box in the basement has died. The cameras on the walls and the coax inside them can keep working for years, and replacing just the recorder is far cheaper and less disruptive than pulling new cable through an occupied city building. Here's how to tell what you have, which recorder to move to, and what to budget for a DVR Upgrade NYC.

Step 1: confirm it's the recorder

These signs point at the recorder rather than the cameras:

  • It restarts on its own, and the timeline has gaps overnight.
  • The screen says "No HDD," or footage only reaches back a day or two.
  • The phone app stopped connecting years ago and never came back — manufacturers retire the cloud servers older recorders depend on.
  • Several channels went black at once, or the unit runs hot with a silent fan.
  • The model is discontinued and gets no firmware updates.

If one camera is dark and the rest are fine, look at that camera and its cable first. If everything dropped together, the recorder or its power supply is the likely culprit.

Step 2: identify what's plugged into it

Look at the back of the box. Round twist-lock BNC connectors mean coax cameras and a DVR. Square RJ45 network jacks mean IP cameras and an NVR. Most systems installed in NYC buildings before the mid-2010s are coax. Coax cameras also come in different signal types — TVI, CVI, AHD and old standard-definition analog — and a replacement recorder has to read each one. Modern multi-format recorders detect the type per channel, which matters when a building added cameras a few at a time over a decade.

Abstract Enterprises Security Systems defaults to the hybrid for most NYC upgrades and says it typically costs only $20–$80 more than a plain DVR. The logic is staging: swap the dead box now so every analog camera records again, then retire the old cameras gradually as each one wears out, each new IP unit landing on the same recorder.

Step 4: size the storage for how far back you need to look

Retention is where an old recorder lets owners down. A landlord, insurer or the police asks for footage from last week, and a worn drive on old compression has already overwritten it. New recorders use H.265-family compression and larger surveillance-rated drives. As a rough guide from the Abstract Enterprises Security Systems page, four 4MP cameras recording on motion fit roughly a month on a 2TB drive; set them to record around the clock and that shrinks to about a week. Storage grows in step with camera count and resolution, so twice the cameras means about twice the drive.

New York doesn't set a blanket retention period for most private residential buildings, so the number usually comes from your own policy, your insurer or your board. Many buildings aim for 30 to 90 days. Plug your camera count into the storage calculator to see what a given drive actually delivers.

Step 5: check what can sink a DIY swap

A careful owner can swap a recorder on a simple, single-format system. Three traps catch people in older buildings:

  • The power supply. A tired camera power supply that stressed the old box can damage the new one too.
  • The cable. Solid-copper RG59 usually carries HD analog fine; thin kit cable from big-box systems sometimes needs re-terminating. Corroded BNC ends make channels read "no signal."
  • Remote access. Old systems often relied on exposed port-forwarding. A current recorder with its own app and view-only user accounts is safer — give the super playback without admin rights.

Where the recorder lives changes the job

In a Manhattan co-op the box often sits in a locked telecom closet and the work needs board approval and a certificate of insurance. In Brooklyn brownstones and Queens two-families it's usually in a cellar, where an old renovation may have nicked a run. Bronx walk-ups and busy commercial strips run recorders hard with near-constant motion, which eats storage. Wherever it is, nobody checks it until an incident — which is why the most common discovery is a recorder that stopped saving weeks ago.

A short scenario

Consider a corner deli in Brooklyn with six coax cameras and a DVR that reboots every night. The cameras test fine, so the owner keeps them, moves to an 8-channel hybrid with a larger surveillance drive, and adds one IP camera over the register later — no new cable through the store, and phone viewing works again the same day.

FAQ

Do I have to buy new cameras when the DVR dies?

Usually not. Most HD coax cameras work with a new recorder over the existing cable once the signal type is matched; multi-format recorders detect it per channel.

What does a DVR upgrade cost in NYC?

The Abstract Enterprises Security Systems NYC page lists roughly $450–$700 for a 4-channel swap, $650–$1,000 for 8 channels, $800–$1,200 for an 8-channel hybrid, and $1,200–$2,200 for 16-channel commercial jobs, with final pricing after on-site testing.

Should I replace my DVR with an NVR?

If your coax cameras are healthy, a hybrid recorder keeps them and leaves room for IP cameras later. An NVR makes sense when the cameras are failing anyway or you need 4K, plate or face detail on every channel.

Why does my new DVR show no signal on some cameras?

Usually a signal-type mismatch or a faulty cable or connector. A recorder set to auto-detect clears the mismatch; a bad run needs re-terminating.

How much storage do I need for 30 days?

It depends on camera count and resolution. As a rough guide, a 2TB drive gives four 4MP cameras on motion recording roughly a month with modern compression.

How long does a DVR last?

Typically five to eight years. The hard drive usually fails first because it writes around the clock.
