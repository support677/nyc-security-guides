When doing it yourself is reasonable here

Some Island houses are friendly to a homeowner with a ladder. A one-story ranch or a cape with a walkable attic lets you drop cable straight down into the soffit, and every camera position is reachable from a six-foot step. Another easy case is a house that already has coax in the walls from an old analog system: if the cable tests good, newer HD-analog cameras and a fresh DVR can go on the same runs without opening a wall.

The harder cases are just as common. A center-hall colonial puts the eave camera 20 feet up. A garage at the back of a 150-foot driveway needs a cable buried, run overhead or fed from its own switch. Standard Ethernet tops out at 100 meters (about 328 feet) per run, so a far outbuilding often gets a small PoE switch of its own fed by one cable from the house. If any of that sounds unfamiliar, the installed route usually costs less than a second trip to fix the first attempt.

The parts list, line by line

  1. Cameras. Choose the body for the mounting spot. Turrets have no clear bubble, so nothing fogs or throws back infrared under a soffit. Bullets have more reach and look like a warning from the road. Domes are harder to knock out of aim where someone can touch them. Choose resolution by distance: 2K is plenty at a front door, while the end of a long driveway or a boat slip earns 4K.
  2. Recorder. Buy one size up. A four-channel box fills up the first time someone wants the shed or the pool gate covered, so most detached houses are better served by eight channels.
  3. Surveillance hard drive. A desktop drive is not built to write around the clock. As a rough estimate only: six cameras at 3 Mbps each write about 194 GB a day, so a month of continuous video needs close to 6 TB, which points to an 8 TB drive. Motion-only recording stretches that. Try different numbers in the storage calculator.
  4. PoE switch. Adds ports when the recorder runs out, and solves the detached garage by putting a short run at the far end.
  5. Junction boxes and mounts. A box behind the camera keeps the splice out of the rain; an arm or corner mount gets the lens past a gutter.
  6. Outdoor cable and connectors. Outdoor-rated jacket, drip loops, and sealed or greased connectors. Copper-clad aluminum cable is cheaper and a poor bet for long PoE runs.
  7. Battery backup. A UPS for the recorder, the switch and the modem. Without one, the system records nothing for as long as the power is out.

Bay air, connectors and the coast

Houses near the bays in places like Freeport, Babylon or the Hamptons, and along the North Fork, see salt in the air most of the year. The camera body usually survives; the connector behind it does not. Exposed network and coax ends turn green, video starts dropping in wet weather, and the camera gets blamed. The fix is in how the job is put together: connections inside a junction box, dielectric grease on the contacts, stainless hardware, and the cable entering from below so water runs off rather than in. Sound-side properties tend to see gentler exposure than ocean-side ones, but the same habits pay off there too.

Storm season and the power question

Long Island has a long memory for outages. Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020 left hundreds of thousands of PSEG Long Island customers in the dark, some for days. A camera system rides that out only if three things stay powered: the recorder, the PoE switch feeding the cameras, and the internet equipment if you want to watch remotely. A UPS sized to those three, rather than the whole house, is a modest add-on. If the house has a standby generator, put the recorder's outlet on a protected circuit.

A seasonal house that sits empty half the year

Close to half of the homes in the Town of Southampton are seasonal, according to the town's own housing plan, and the pattern repeats across the East End. An empty house changes the shopping list:

  • Keep the internet service active over the winter; a cancelled plan means no alerts.
  • Record on site, so footage survives an internet outage and nobody needs a cloud subscription.
  • Give a caretaker or neighbor their own viewer login instead of sharing yours.
  • Turn on drive-health and camera-offline notifications, and open the app once a week.
  • Point one camera at the driveway entrance, not just the door, since a vehicle is what tells you who was there.

Five mistakes we are called out to fix

  2. Cameras aimed straight down from the eave, showing the tops of heads.
  3. A recorder in a closet with no ventilation, overheating every July.
  4. One shared password for the whole family and a former contractor.

Frequently asked questions

Can I buy security cameras on Long Island without installation?

Yes. Equipment-only orders from Abstract Enterprises Security Systems are delivered to homes and businesses in Nassau and Suffolk. There is no pickup counter, and delivery timing is confirmed when you order.

How much does an installed camera system cost on Long Island?

A four-camera house-brand package, fully installed with its recorder and cabling, begins at $999. Larger systems and individual parts are priced by call or text because equipment prices change.

Will a Wi-Fi camera work on a detached garage?

Often not well. Wi-Fi cameras still need power, and a garage far from the router loses signal. A wired PoE camera, or a small PoE switch in the garage fed by one cable, is more dependable.

Do the cameras record during a blackout?

Yes, if the recorder and the PoE switch are on battery backup; put the modem and router on it too if you want to watch remotely. Cameras powered over PoE run as long as the switch does.

How big a hard drive do I need?

It depends on camera count, resolution and bitrate. As a rough example, six cameras at 3 Mbps recording continuously need close to 6 TB for 30 days, so an 8 TB surveillance drive fits. Motion-only recording lasts longer.

Is there a monthly fee?

Not for local recording. The recorder on your property stores the video and you view it through its phone app. Some name brands sell optional cloud plans, but none is needed to record.
