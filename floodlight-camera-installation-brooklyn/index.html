Floodlight Camera Installation Brooklyn work goes best when you plan the light first and the camera second. A floodlight cam is the one security device everyone on the block sees every night, and on a twenty-foot-wide rowhouse lot the glare that protects your yard can just as easily land in the bedroom across the fence. This guide walks through sketching the beam, choosing a power route for a brick back wall, keeping the neighbors on side, and what the visit itself looks like.

Sketch the beam on paper before anything is ordered

Walk to the back fence, or the street end of the driveway, and turn to face the house. Then answer three questions, ideally with a rough drawing:

  1. What must be lit? Usually a garden-floor door, the gate to the side alley, the trash area or a basement stair. Mark each one.
  2. Where does your property end? Draw the fence lines. In most of Brownsville, Bed-Stuy, Flatbush and Sunset Park the yard is narrow and boxed in by brick, so light bounces and a beam that seems modest on the box ends up much brighter in practice.
  3. Whose windows face the yard? The rear windows of the houses behind and beside you are often only a couple of dozen feet away. Mark them; the lamps and the motion zone must stay clear of them.

That sketch decides more than the brand ever will. A short, enclosed yard usually needs one fixture angled down toward the door, set to motion with a short timer. A detached house in Marine Park, Mill Basin or Dyker Heights with a long driveway often does better with two moderate lights, one near the side door and one toward the garage, than with a single very bright unit trying to reach both ends.

Most Brooklyn rear walls are plain common brick with either an old porch-light box over the garden door or nothing at all. The power route sets the price range more than anything else.

Keeping the block friendly once the light is up

Complaints about bright security lights come up regularly on the Brooklyn subreddit, and the usual advice there is to call 311 or the local Council member. Four settings prevent almost every dispute:

  • Tilt the heads down. Lamps aimed level throw light over the fence. Aim them at the ground in front of the door.
  • Motion, not dusk-to-dawn, for homes. A short on-time after motion lights the yard when something happens and leaves it dark the rest of the night. Businesses with an empty lot are the exception.
  • Draw the motion zone to your fence. If the neighbor's cat or the neighbor themselves trips your light, the zone is too big.
  • Warmer, lower output. Most floodlight cameras are rated for suburban lots. In an enclosed rowhouse yard a fraction of the maximum is usually plenty, and a warmer colour is easier on the windows around you.

The camera side matters legally too. Under Civil Rights Law § 52-a, a New York homeowner or renter whose backyard is filmed from the property next door without their written consent can take the camera owner to court. Privacy masking on the edge of the frame takes a few minutes and settles the question.

The front of the house: landmarks and the street

Most Brooklyn floodlight jobs are at the back for a reason. Avenue and side-street fronts already get LED streetlight, and many brownstone and limestone fronts sit in historic districts such as Brooklyn Heights, Park Slope or Clinton Hill. Under the Landmarks Preservation Commission's staff criteria, a landmarked front gets at most two light fixtures and one intercom panel at each door, placed in or right next to the opening. A full twin-head floodlight on a carved front is not going to read as discreet. For the front, a small light-and-camera fixture beside the door, or simply a doorbell camera, is the realistic choice.

What install day looks like on a rowhouse back

  1. Check the box or the wall. An old porch-light box gets tested for depth and fixing before anything heavier hangs from it. On bare brick the mounting plate goes into mortar with proper anchors.
  2. Hold the light up at night height. The installer holds the fixture in place while you watch the live view on your phone, so you agree on what it sees before any holes are drilled.
  3. Prove the Wi-Fi at the wall. In a long, narrow house with the router at the front or in the cellar, the back wall can sit at the edge of the signal. If the phone shows one bar at the garden door, a wired access point or a PoE camera is the fix, not a different floodlight.
  4. Seal it. Cable entries through brick get sealed so water does not run into the wall or the box.
  5. Set it with you. Light timer, motion zones, privacy masks, who in the household gets alerts, and where clips are kept, ideally locally on an SD card or your own recorder when the model supports it.

Before you book: what to send the installer

  • Two pictures: the whole back wall shot from the fence or the end of the driveway, plus a close shot of what is mounted there today, whether that is an old porch light, an outlet or plain brick.
  • Whether the existing rear light is on an inside switch, and where that switch is.
  • Who shares the yard or alley, and whether a tenant or neighbor has raised light before.
  • Whether the house is in a historic district, if the job touches the front.
  • Your landlord's written OK if you rent; the outside wall is theirs.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an electrician for a floodlight camera in Brooklyn?

For the 120-volt side, yes. The NYC Department of Buildings treats even replacing a fixture on an existing outlet as electrical work that needs a permit and a licensed master electrician. A low-voltage installer handles the camera, network and app, and a PoE spotlight camera can avoid new 120-volt work altogether.

How much does floodlight camera installation cost in Brooklyn?

On the Abstract Enterprises Security Systems Brooklyn page, mounting onto a sound existing outdoor light is listed at $300 to $575 plus the camera; a wall with no power yet is listed at $575 to $1,500.

My neighbor says my floodlight shines into their bedroom. What can I change?

Tilt the lamp heads down, switch the light from dusk-to-dawn to motion with a short timer, shrink the motion zone to your own fence line and lower the brightness. Those four changes usually end the complaint without removing the light.

Can my floodlight camera record the yard next door?

It should not. Under New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a, filming a neighbor's backyard without their written consent can get you sued, so mask the edges of the frame.

Why does my rear floodlight camera keep going offline at night?

Usually one of two things: someone switches off the inside wall switch that feeds it, or the back wall is at the edge of the Wi-Fi from a front or cellar router. A switch guard and a wired access point or PoE camera fix those.
