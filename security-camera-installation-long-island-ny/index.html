Think in zones, not cameras

The most common mistake on suburban lots is buying a box of four cameras and then hunting for places to stick them. Start the other way around: walk the property and list every way a person or vehicle can reach the house. Each of those approach paths is a zone, and each zone gets the camera type that suits it.

ZoneWhat you want to seeCamera that usually fits
Driveway and curbCars pulling in, plates at the apron, people walking upLong-range bullet or a 4K eyeball aimed down the drive, mounted under the soffit
Front door and porchFaces at close range, package dropsWide dome or eyeball at the door, or a video doorbell paired with the system
Side yards and gatesAnyone walking the narrow strip between housesNarrow-angle bullet looking straight down the side path
Backyard, deck, poolWide overview after darkFull-color night vision or active-deterrent camera with a light and siren
Detached garage or shedDoors and the walk between garage and houseBullet on the garage fed by a buried or overhead cable run

Count the zones and you have your real camera count. For most Long Island homes that ends up between six and ten — more than people expect, which is why planning first saves money later.

Wired or Wi-Fi on a big lot

Battery and Wi-Fi cameras are tempting because they skip the cabling. On a suburban property the trade-offs show up fast.

Wired PoEWi-Fi / battery
Signal to the back fence or garageNot an issue — one cable per cameraOften weak through brick, siding and distance
PowerDelivered over the same cableBatteries to recharge or an outlet at every mount
RecordingContinuous to a recorder inside the houseOften clips only, frequently tied to a cloud plan
Monthly costNone for local recordingCommon for storage and history
Upfront laborHigher — cables go through attic, basement or soffitLower

The wiring is the part homeowners dread, but on colonials, capes, split-levels and ranches the cable usually rides through the attic and drops behind the soffit, or comes up from the basement. Nothing should be stapled across the siding. Detached garages need a planned path, either overhead or in conduit, and that run is often the single biggest variable in the quote.

Long Island conditions that change the hardware

Salt air and storm exposure

Homes near the water from Long Beach to the East End live with salt spray and nor'easter wind. Pick cameras rated IP66 or better, insist on corrosion-resistant mounting screws, and make sure every cable entry has a drip loop so water runs off instead of into the wall. Plan for surge protection where long outdoor runs come back into the house.

Dark streets

Plenty of Suffolk and North Shore roads have little or no street lighting. Infrared night vision handles darkness, but a full-color camera with its own light gives footage you can actually use to describe clothing or a car color.

Neighbors close on one side, far on the other

Older Nassau neighborhoods can have houses ten feet apart, while a Suffolk lot may back onto woods. Aim side-yard cameras down your own path rather than across a neighbor's windows, and use privacy masking in the recorder if a lens catches more than you want.

What drives the price

The Abstract Enterprises Security Systems page for this area starts 4-camera packages at $999 (recorder, cabling, labor and two weeks of stored footage are part of that price) and puts 4–8 camera residential systems at $1,117 to $2,500. Where your job lands inside or above that range comes down to a few things:

  • Camera count — set by your zones, not by a package size.
  • Cable distance — a far garage or back fence adds material and labor.
  • Camera grade — 1080p, 2K and 4K, and whether you want full-color night vision or active deterrent.
  • Recording length — more days of history means a bigger drive. Run your own numbers in the storage calculator.
  • Add-ons — doing an intercom or video doorbell in the same visit shares the wiring work.

Install day, step by step

  1. Walk-through. The tech confirms each zone, marks mount points, and checks where the recorder will live — usually a closet, basement or near the router.
  2. Cable routing. Attic and basement access first; this is the longest part of the day.
  3. Mounting. Cameras go under eaves where possible to keep rain off the lens.
  4. Recorder setup. Hard drive formatted, motion zones drawn, privacy masks set.
  5. Phone app. Live view and playback set up on your phone before the crew leaves.

Questions to ask any installer

  • Will every camera be hardwired, and where exactly will the cables run?
  • Where does the footage live, and is there any monthly fee to see it on my phone?
  • How many days of continuous recording will the drive hold at the resolution you're quoting?
  • What happens to outdoor connections near the water — gaskets, drip loops, surge protection?
  • Can you put the whole plan in writing so my HOA or board can review it?

FAQ

How many cameras does a typical Long Island house need?

Most single-family homes in Nassau and Suffolk land between six and ten cameras once the driveway, both side yards, the backyard and any detached garage are counted. A small cape on a tight lot may need four; a corner property or a home with a pool and long driveway needs more.

Is Wi-Fi good enough for cameras on a suburban lot, or should they be wired?

Wired PoE cameras are the safer choice. A router inside the house rarely reaches a detached garage or the back fence reliably, and every wireless camera still needs power at the mount. One Ethernet cable per camera carries both power and video to a recorder indoors.

Do I need a permit or HOA approval to put up cameras on Long Island?

Standard low-voltage camera work on a private home usually does not need a building permit, but condo boards, co-ops and many HOAs have rules about exterior fixtures and where they can mount. Get the written rule from your board before install day and keep cameras aimed at your own property.

Can I record audio on my outdoor cameras?

Be careful. New York's eavesdropping law restricts recording conversations you are not part of, so many owners leave audio off on cameras that face a sidewalk or a neighbor's yard and use it only for two-way talk at their own door.

How much does professional camera installation cost on Long Island?

Abstract Enterprises prices its entry 4-camera package from $999 — that covers the recorder, cabling, labor and two weeks of recording — and lists residential systems of four to eight cameras in the $1,117 to $2,500 range. Lot size, cable distance and camera type move the final number.

Will salt air near the South Shore ruin outdoor cameras?

Coastal air is hard on cheap hardware. Choose cameras with an IP66 or IP67 rating, stainless or coated mounting hardware, and drip loops on every cable entry, and wipe the lenses a few times a year if you live near the water.
