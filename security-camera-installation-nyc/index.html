Security Camera Installation NYC: A Building-by-Building Planning Guide

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-07

Security Camera Installation NYC projects rarely go wrong because of the camera. They go wrong because of the building: a limestone facade nobody wants drilled, a co-op board that meets once a month, a recorder location four floors away from the back yard, or a Wi-Fi signal that gives up at the first brick wall. This guide walks through those decisions in the order you will actually hit them. If you already know what you want and just need current package prices, the Security Camera Installation NYC page from Abstract Enterprises Security Systems lists them.

Step one: figure out who has to say yes

In New York the first question is less "which camera" and more "whose wall is it." The answer changes the timeline more than any equipment choice.

  • A house you own. Your call. In a historic district, keep exterior hardware small and the wiring out of sight, since facade changes there can fall under Landmarks Preservation Commission rules.
  • A rental apartment. Inside your unit is generally up to you, but drilling or running cable usually falls under the lease's alteration clause. Hallways, lobbies and the facade belong to the owner.
  • A co-op. Corridors and the exterior are common areas the board controls. Plan for an alteration agreement, a certificate of insurance from the installer, and work limited to the hours the building allows.
  • A condo. The bylaws normally treat hallways and facades as common elements; the managing agent can tell you what needs sign-off.
  • A store, office or mixed-use building. The landlord may need to approve exterior mounts, and New York requires employers to give staff written notice of electronic monitoring.

Where cameras can and can't point

The statute that matters is New York Penal Law §250.45, unlawful surveillance. Bathrooms, changing areas and the inside of someone else's home are off limits without consent. Lobbies, mailrooms, elevators, stairwells, garages and building exteriors are generally fine for an owner to cover, and the public sidewalk is public. Hallway cameras should look down the corridor rather than into apartment doorways.

Audio is the gray zone. New York lets you record a conversation you are part of, but a microphone quietly picking up strangers talking in a hallway is a different question, which is why most professional installs leave audio off in common areas. This is general information, not legal advice; for a dispute, talk to your managing agent or a lawyer.

What actually moves the price

  • How many cameras, and how many floors or walls each cable has to cross.
  • The mounting surface: brick, limestone, vinyl siding, a drop ceiling or a steel storefront gate box.
  • Camera features: 4K resolution, color night vision, built-in sirens and strobes, two-way talk.
  • Recorder size, which sets how many days of footage you keep.
  • Scheduling limits, such as a co-op that only allows weekday work.

How many days of footage do you need?

Most problems are noticed late: a missing package, a scraped car, an argument with a contractor about when they showed up. Two weeks of recording is a sensible floor for a home. Businesses and buildings that deal with insurance claims often want thirty days or more. The drive you need depends on camera count, resolution, frame rate and whether you record nonstop or only on motion. A typical install day

  1. Walk-through. Confirm every view with you (and the super, if there is one), and pick a recorder spot with power and internet — a closet, basement shelf or under a desk.
  2. Cable pulls. Through basements, risers, closets and existing holes where possible; color-matched raceway outside only when there is no interior route.
  3. Mounting. Anchors set into mortar joints instead of face brick where the building prefers it, and drip loops so water can't follow the cable inside.
  4. Recorder setup. Drive formatted, motion zones drawn so a busy sidewalk doesn't trigger alerts all afternoon.
  5. Handoff. The app on your phone, a test playback, and a lesson on exporting a clip — the step everyone forgets until the day they need it.

A four-to-eight camera home usually fits in a single working day. Apartment buildings that want lobby, hallway and perimeter coverage can stretch to one to three days.

Questions to ask any installer

  • Are you licensed by New York State for low-voltage work, and can you send a certificate of insurance to my board or management company?
  • Who owns the recorder and the admin password after you leave?
  • Is there any monthly fee, cloud plan or contract?
  • If a camera dies in month two, what is covered and for how long?
  • Exactly where will the cable run, and will any of it show on the outside of the building?
  • Can I export footage myself for the police or my insurer?

A quick example

Picture a brick two-family house in Queens with a detached garage at the back of the lot. Wi-Fi cameras keep dropping at the rear. A wired layout puts the recorder in the basement beside the router and runs cable inside to the stoop, along the basement ceiling to the yard, and out to the garage in conduit: four cameras, nothing visible on the facade, nothing to recharge.

Frequently asked questions

What does security camera installation cost in NYC?

On the Abstract Enterprises page, residential systems of four to eight cameras run about $850 to $2,500 installed, and commercial systems start around $1,500 and go past $5,000. Cable distance, number of floors crossed, mounting surface and camera features move the final number, which comes after an on-site look.

Can my landlord put security cameras in my building's hallways?

Generally yes. Lobbies, hallways, elevators, stairwells and other common areas are spaces where tenants don't have a reasonable expectation of privacy. What New York's unlawful surveillance law (Penal Law 250.45) prohibits is recording in bathrooms, changing areas or inside a tenant's apartment without consent.

Can I install a camera outside my apartment door in NYC?

No state or city law bans it, but the hallway is a common area, so your lease, co-op board or condo bylaws decide. Ask management first. A peephole-style camera that replaces existing hardware usually draws the fewest objections.

Do I need a permit to install security cameras in NYC?

Typical low-voltage camera work on a home or small building usually doesn't need a building permit, but approvals still apply: co-op and condo boards, landlords for common areas, and Landmarks rules for facades in historic districts. Adding new electrical outlets is separate work for an electrician.

Should I choose wired or Wi-Fi cameras for a New York building?

In brick, concrete and steel buildings, wired PoE is the more dependable pick: one cable carries power and video, so there is no signal to lose and no battery to swap. Wi-Fi cameras make sense for renters who can't drill or for a single indoor view close to the router.

Is there a monthly fee for a professionally installed camera system?

Not if footage records to a recorder on site. The systems on the Abstract Enterprises page record locally with remote phone viewing included and list $0 monthly fees. Cloud-based consumer cameras are the ones that usually carry subscriptions.
