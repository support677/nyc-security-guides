Access Control Installation NYC: Fobs, Cards, Phones and the Rules That Come With Them

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-07

Access Control Installation NYC jobs look like hardware projects, but most of the hard decisions are about people: who gets a credential, how fast you can take one away, and what happens to the door when the power or the internet goes out. Hardware follows from those answers. For package examples and current price ranges, Abstract Enterprises Security Systems keeps an Access Control Installation NYC page.

The four parts of every system

Strip away the brand names and every setup has the same four pieces. A credential (fob, card, PIN, phone or fingerprint). A reader at the door. A controller, usually in a closet or basement, that decides yes or no. And a lock — an electric strike or magnetic lock — that actually releases. Upgrades often swap only one or two of these. A building with sound door hardware and wiring might only need new readers and a new controller, which is far cheaper than starting over.

Tenant forums such as r/NYCapartments are a useful reality check here. The complaints are rarely about technology. People object to steep replacement fees for a lost fob, strict limits on how many fobs a household gets, and the fear of being locked out if a system or account fails. A good design plans for those moments up front: a clear policy on extra credentials, and a mechanical key or override for when things go wrong.

Fail-safe, fail-secure, and getting out

Every electrified door has to answer one question: what does it do when power is cut? A fail-safe lock (magnetic locks are always this type) unlocks. A fail-secure lock, typical of many electric strikes, stays locked from the outside. Egress doors on a path out of the building must let people leave freely, without a key, card or code, and magnetic locks need fire alarm tie-in and a manual release on the inside. Interior storage or server rooms can be fail-secure. The Abstract Enterprises Security Systems page describes sizing battery backup for four to eight hours, which matters in a city where a block-wide outage can last an evening.

Tenant data and keyless entry

If you own a residential building, cloud and biometric systems come with obligations. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Act sets rules for how owners of many multiple dwellings collect, store and share data from keyless entry systems, including limits on what can be gathered and who can see it. Ask any vendor how long access logs are kept, who can read them, and whether the platform uses facial or fingerprint data before you sign. Treat this as general information rather than legal advice.

Door hardware condition is the wild card. A warped frame, a missing power source near the door, or a landmarked entrance that limits what can be mounted outside will push a job toward the top of its range.

How an install usually runs

  1. Door survey. Each opening is checked for frame condition, which way it swings, the lock already there, and where power can come from.
  2. Credential plan. Who gets what, which doors, which hours, and how lost credentials get reported and cancelled.
  3. Rough-in. Cable from each door to the controller, kept inside the building where the walls allow.
  4. Hardware. Strikes or maglocks, readers, request-to-exit buttons or sensors, door position contacts.
  5. Programming and enrollment. Schedules set, credentials issued, and a test of every door with power on and power off.
  6. Handoff. Someone on your side learns to add and remove users before the installer leaves.

A single keypad and new strike can go in within a few hours. Networked multi-door systems take longer, mostly because of programming and enrollment.

Pairing it with the intercom and cameras

In most NYC apartment buildings the front door already has an intercom-driven release, so access control and the intercom share the same strike and often the same cable path. Planning them together avoids two crews and two sets of holes. See the intercom installation guide for the visitor side, and add a camera over the reader if you want every entry tied to an image.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • Are the credentials encrypted, and can they be copied with cheap consumer tools?
  • Is there a monthly software fee, and what happens to the doors if we stop paying?
  • Which doors are fail-safe and which are fail-secure, and why?
  • How long does the battery backup last?
  • Who owns the admin account and the access logs?
  • Can we keep existing wiring and door hardware anywhere?

Frequently asked questions

What does access control installation cost in NYC?

Published Abstract Enterprises Security Systems ranges run $1,500 to $3,000 per door for standalone keypad or card systems, about $3,000 to $8,000 to move a 30-unit building off old fobs, $8,000 to $18,000 for an office of around 40 people, and $15,000 to $75,000 for commercial buildings with 10 to 50 doors.

Can apartment key fobs be copied?

Older 125kHz proximity fobs have no encryption and can be duplicated with inexpensive handheld copiers, which is why many NYC buildings are moving to encrypted 13.56MHz cards and fobs or mobile credentials that are far harder to clone.

Can my landlord charge me for an extra or replacement key fob?

Many NYC leases set a fee for lost or extra fobs, and it's one of the most common complaints in tenant forums. What's allowed can depend on your lease and whether the unit is rent-regulated, so check the lease and ask HPD or a tenant organization if a charge seems unreasonable.

What happens to electronic door locks during a power outage?

It depends on how each door is configured. Fail-safe locks, including all magnetic locks, release when power is lost; fail-secure locks stay locked from outside. Exit doors must always let people leave, and battery backup keeps the system running for a set number of hours.

Do I need a licensed contractor to install access control in NYC?

For shared buildings and commercial spaces, yes in practice: the work involves low-voltage wiring, door hardware tied to egress and fire alarms, and often insurance or board requirements. A consumer smart lock on a single apartment door is a different, simpler job.

Can access control work with our existing intercom?

Usually. Many lobby intercoms already drive the door strike, and fob or card readers can share that strike and the same cable route. Some smart intercom panels include a built-in reader, so visitors and residents use one device at the door.
