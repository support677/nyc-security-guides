Residential Security Camera Installation Queens jobs are shaped less by the camera you pick than by the shape of the lot it goes on. A corner colonial in Bayside has two street faces and a long driveway. A Ridgewood attached row has a stoop in front, a yard in back and nothing in between. A Woodside semi-detached may share a driveway strip with the house next door. A Rego Park garden co-op unit has one entry and a board that controls the facade. This guide works through those lot types, then the decisions that come after placement: where the recorder sits, how long footage is kept, and what happens when the power drops. Corner and detached lots

Two street faces mean two directions of approach, and most owners underestimate the side street. The camera that matters most is usually the one aimed at the curb cut, with a narrower lens so a plate is readable, rather than a wide lens trying to take in the whole block. A garage at the end of a long driveway is where Wi-Fi gives up; plan a wired run along the house and fence line, or in conduit if the yard is being redone anyway.

Attached rows

From the street a row house is watched by everyone on the block. The rear is the opposite: a fenced yard backing onto other yards, with no one looking. Put the effort there. Cable usually leaves the basement through the back wall, and a camera with full-color night mode earns its price on a dark yard lit only by a kitchen window.

Garden co-ops and condo units

Expect written board approval before anything is drilled into a hallway or facade. Where that is a long process, a peephole camera or an indoor camera looking out a window covers the entry without touching common property. Forest Hills Gardens runs its own architectural review through its private corporation, and Jackson Heights sits in a historic district, so exterior changes there take more paperwork than the same job a mile away.

Shared driveways: settle it before the drill comes out

Side driveways split between semi-detached houses are a Queens fixture, and so are the arguments over them. A camera can end an argument or start a new one. A sequence that tends to keep the peace:

  1. Know the line. Your survey or deed shows who owns which strip. Most cameras end up covering both strips anyway, so be ready to explain why.
  2. Talk first. Tell the neighbor what the camera will see and offer to show them the view once it is set up.
  3. Mask their side. Privacy masking blacks out their windows, side door and yard in the recording, so what is saved is the shared pavement and your property.
  4. Aim at your own car and door. A camera pointed at their entry reads as surveillance, even when it is not meant that way.
  5. Leave audio off outdoors. New York's eavesdropping law makes it a crime to record a conversation you are not part of, and a driveway mic catches exactly that.

If you own a two-family, you are also a landlord. Cover the shared entry, the driveway and the basement door, never the inside of a tenant's unit, and tell the tenant in writing which areas are recorded. That one page heads off most disputes.

Where the recorder lives, and how cable reaches it

In most Queens houses the recorder goes in the basement, close to the router. The cable then leaves through the rim joist for ground-floor cameras and climbs through the attic and soffits for the upper ones, which is why attic access shortens a job. The siding matters at the drill point: aluminum and vinyl siding, stucco and face brick each take a different bit and a different seal.

Near Jamaica Bay, ask for corrosion-rated housings and hardware, and note that south of the Belt Parkway the basement rule changes. Many Howard Beach and Rockaway basements took water in Hurricane Sandy, so put the recorder on a high shelf or in a first-floor closet and run the cable down to it. A recorder sitting on the slab is the first thing lost in a flood, and the footage goes with it.

Keep the permanent cameras wired. Power over Ethernet sends power and video down one cable, and it stays up where a Wi-Fi camera on the far side of a brick wall keeps dropping.

Storage, outages and who gets the app

Most homes keep about 30 days of footage on a local recorder, more with a bigger drive. Motion-based recording stretches it further. To check the math for your own camera count and resolution, run the numbers in the security camera storage calculator.

A UPS battery on the recorder and its PoE switch keeps every wired camera recording through a blackout, because the cameras draw power from that box. When the internet drops, a local recorder keeps recording; only the phone view stops until service returns.

Decide who gets viewer access before install day: each adult in the house, a grown child who checks in on a parent, and, in a two-family, possibly the tenant for the shared entry camera only. Keep the admin password with the owner.

Reading the published price bands

The Queens page gives all-in ranges: a co-op or garden apartment with one to three cameras runs $1,062 to $2,000; an attached or two-family home with four to eight cameras, $2,000 to $4,500; a detached single-family with five to ten cameras, $2,500 to $6,000; larger, three-family and waterfront homes are quoted custom. None of them carries a monthly charge when the footage stays on a recorder in the house. Where a job lands inside its band depends on cable length to the garage, whether there is attic access, how many separate entrances there are, and whether you add a plate camera or an active-deterrence unit at the porch. The final number is fixed in writing after a free walk-through.

Five questions before you sign

  1. Which cameras are wired, which are wireless, and why?
  2. Where will the recorder sit, and how high off the basement floor?
  3. Will you show me the masked view of my neighbor's side before you leave?
  4. Who holds the admin password, and can family or a tenant get view-only access?
  5. What exactly is in the written price, and what would be extra?

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal in NYC to have a backyard camera that also sees the neighbors' yards?

Recording areas visible from your own property is generally allowed, but aiming at a neighbor's windows or private yard invites a complaint. Use privacy masking to black out their side so the recording shows only your yard.

Can my driveway camera record my neighbor's car on a shared driveway?

A camera covering a shared driveway will see both cars. Talk to the neighbor first, mask their windows and door, aim at your own car and entry, and keep audio recording off.

Do I have to tell my upstairs tenant about the cameras?

Tell them in writing which shared areas are recorded, such as the entry, driveway and basement door. Cameras should never look into a tenant's unit, and audio should stay off in shared spaces.

How many cameras does a Queens two-family house need?

Four to eight is the usual range: front entry, driveway, side alley or path, backyard and the basement or side entrance. The walk-through settles the exact spots.

Will the cameras keep recording if the power or internet goes out?

With a UPS battery on the recorder and PoE switch, wired cameras keep recording through a blackout for a while. A local recorder keeps recording during an internet outage; only remote viewing pauses.
