Aim at your property, not the house next door

Search any Long Island forum and you will find a thread about a neighbor's camera pointed into someone's backyard. Avoiding that argument is mostly about aiming and settings:

  • Point side-yard cameras along your own fence line rather than across it, and angle the driveway camera to end at the property edge.
  • Use privacy masks in the recorder to black out a neighbor's windows, pool or yard that cannot be kept out of frame.
  • Leave microphones off on cameras that face the sidewalk or a shared driveway. Under New York's eavesdropping statute, capturing a conversation you aren't part of can be a criminal offense, and a camera that picks up voices on the street can cross that line. Ask a lawyer if you are unsure about a particular camera.
  • If you share a driveway on a two-family lot, talk to the other household before the install, and show them the view.

South Shore water, North Shore distance

The two coasts of the county push camera systems in opposite directions.

On the South Shore, the recorder is the piece to protect. Many canal and bay-front homes took water in their basements during Hurricane Sandy, and a recorder on a basement shelf holds every hour of footage you own. Put it on the first floor or higher, in a closet with some airflow, and plug it into a battery backup so a short outage does not wipe the last few minutes of video. Outdoors, salt air eats cheap mounts and connectors; stainless or sealed housings and weatherproof junction boxes last longer near the water.

On the North Shore, the problem is reach. A standard network cable carries power and video about 328 feet, and a gate at the end of a long drive is often farther than that from the house. The usual fixes are a weatherproof switch at a midpoint, fiber to the gate, or a point-to-point wireless link. Mature trees also matter: a view that is clear in March can be full of leaves by June, so plan camera angles with summer foliage in mind.

Get the house ready before install day

  1. Clear a path to the attic hatch, the basement ceiling and the spot where the recorder will live.
  2. Know where your internet router is and have the Wi-Fi name and password handy for the app setup.
  3. Decide who gets the app: both owners, a tenant in the second unit, a relative who checks the house in winter.
  4. Trim shrubs or branches that block the views you care about.
  5. If the house is in a co-op, condo or HOA community, check whether the board has rules on exterior fixtures before anyone drills.
  6. If you have old coax from a previous system, say so. The page notes HD analog cameras can often reuse existing coax, while full 4K IP cameras need Cat5e or Cat6.

Frequently asked questions

Can my driveway camera catch part of my neighbor's property?

Some overlap is often unavoidable, but you can keep it small by aiming along your property line and using privacy masks in the recorder to block out a neighbor's windows or yard. Leave audio off on cameras that face shared or public areas.

Do I pay anything monthly after the install?

No. The recorder stores footage at the house, and remote viewing on your phone is included without a subscription.

How long does installation take?

For a typical house with 4–8 cameras, the published estimate is 4–8 hours. Larger multi-unit buildings can take one to three days.

Should the recorder go in the basement?

Not on the South Shore if the basement has ever flooded. A first-floor or upstairs closet with airflow and a battery backup keeps the footage safer.

Can I keep the coax cable from my old cameras?

Often, yes. Existing coax can usually carry HD analog cameras. For full 4K IP quality, new Cat5e or Cat6 cable is needed, though existing cable paths can often be reused.

The published A Series four-camera package with recorder starts at $999, including two weeks of recording and remote access. Higher-resolution and active-deterrent packages start between $1,047 and $1,441.
