How Alarm.com cameras are sold

Alarm.com is a platform, not a store. Its cameras are sold and supported through authorized service providers, and they only stream and record when the account has an active plan that includes video. That has three practical consequences:

  • The company that holds your account, not the installer, sets the monthly fee.
  • Cameras are added to an account, so whoever owns that login controls who sees the footage.
  • Cameras from other brands generally do not appear in the app on a home account. Business accounts can connect certain third-party cameras through Alarm.com's recorder, but only on the right commercial plan.

Find your situation

"We just bought a house that already has Alarm.com cameras"

This is one of the most common questions on homeowner forums. The equipment usually stays registered to the seller's login until the seller closes it or releases the devices. Until that happens, the old owner may still be able to see your driveway. Step one is a new account in your name with a provider; step two is tracing and testing each camera, resetting any that need it, and adding them to your login. Check for a leftover recorder in a closet while you are at it.

"We already have an Alarm.com alarm and want to add cameras"

Call your provider and confirm video is included in your plan before buying anything. Then send the installer the provider's name, a photo of where your router sits, and how to get into the attic or basement. Those three details decide most of the labor.

"We rent in a managed building"

Apartment complexes like the new buildings around the Ronkonkoma station usually forbid drilling exterior walls. Indoor cameras and battery units that stay inside the lease rules are the realistic choice, and in some cases the honest answer is that you can set them up yourself in the app.

"We run a shop, office or flex space"

Businesses that want continuous recording rather than event clips use Alarm.com's commercial recorder with PoE cameras. Exterior mounts on a leased building need written landlord approval, and on Long Island exterior work permits depend on the town or village, so check per address.

How the house changes the install

A postwar Nassau ranch on a concrete slab has no basement, so camera cable travels through the attic and comes down inside the soffit; nobody drills into a slab that may hold radiant heating pipes. A 1970s Suffolk colonial usually gives you a basement for the router side and higher eaves on the camera side, which means a taller ladder and longer runs. Detached garages, common in older Nassau neighborhoods, are the classic stretch: the camera needs power and a connection across open yard or driveway, so either a dedicated run or a battery unit gets quoted on its own line.

Long Island details people miss

  • Alarm registration. Nassau and Suffolk police both require alarm systems to be registered, on different terms. Adding cameras to an alarm account is a good moment to check that your registration is current.
  • Camera registries. Suffolk police run a free, voluntary camera registry, and some Nassau village departments do too. Registering tells police a camera exists; it does not give them access to your feed.
  • Storm outages. Wired cameras stop when the power or internet drops. A battery backup on the router and any PoE switch carries you through a short outage; for long ones, only a generator keeps the system up.
  • Audio. Under New York law only one participant has to agree to a recorded conversation, so an outdoor or register-area microphone that captures strangers talking is a legal question. Many people simply leave audio off.

What happens on install day

  1. Confirm the account, provider and video plan in the app with you.
  2. Walk the property and agree on each camera view, including what neighbors' windows look like from your doors.
  3. Run, seal and test cable before anything is mounted.
  4. Mount, aim and add each camera to your account.
  5. Set recording rules and notifications, then test every camera on your phone before the tech leaves.

If you want 24/7 footage on top of Alarm.com's event clips, size the recorder's drive first with the free security camera storage calculator; a Suffolk property with long driveway views fills a disk faster than you'd expect.

Questions people ask

Can I use Alarm.com cameras without a monitoring plan?

Not as an Alarm.com system. The cameras need an active provider plan with video on it to stream and record in the app. Some newer battery models can record locally to microSD, but plan on keeping the service if you want the cameras to work as intended.

We bought a house with Alarm.com cameras — how do we get control of them?

Have the previous owner's account closed or the devices released, open an account in your name with a provider, then have each camera reset if needed and added to your login. Until then the old account may still see the cameras.

Can I add another brand's cameras to Alarm.com?

On a typical home account, generally no; the app works with Alarm.com cameras. Business accounts can connect some third-party cameras through Alarm.com's commercial recorder on the right commercial plan.

Do I need a permit to add Alarm.com cameras?

Cameras themselves rarely need one, but the alarm side must be registered with Nassau or Suffolk police, and exterior work rules depend on your town or village. Renters and tenants also need landlord approval for exterior mounts.
