Start with the doors nobody photographs

Listing photos show the front entrance. Break-ins, propped doors and code complaints tend to happen around back. Walk the building and list every one of these:

  • The exterior wooden stair to the upper apartments, typically with a keyed lock that hasn't been re-keyed through several tenancies. A keypad or fob lock at the top landing, a light and a door contact make it manageable.
  • The cellar bulkhead. The state Property Maintenance Code, §304.18, puts hatchways on the same list as doors and windows: each needs a security device. A padlock that every oil delivery driver has a key to doesn't meet the spirit of that. A contact on each leaf and a scheduled code are better.
  • The driveway or side door, which often gets more traffic than the front but was never put on the buzzer.
  • A detached laundry or storage building, opened with a key handed out years ago.
  • Hall and stair lighting. Multiple Residence Law §109 requires every public hall, stair and fire-stair to be adequately lighted on every floor. Cameras depend on it too.

When a storm takes the power

Outages here are routine, not rare. In 2026 alone, a September nor'easter left over 19,000 Central Hudson customers dark, storms and heat in early July hit more than 47,000 Orange & Rockland customers, and a June storm cut power to more than 5,000 Con Edison customers in Westchester. For an absent owner, the question is whether you hear about it. A cellular alarm communicator still reports when the cable internet is down, a UPS on the recorder and network gear keeps a record of who came and went, and a battery on the door hardware keeps the entrance in the state you chose. A front door held only by a magnetic lock opens when power fails, so that door needs a battery sized for a long outage or a different lock.

A six-unit frame house in a river city

Picture a six-unit house near a downtown train station: a front door with an original buzzer that rings in two of the six apartments, a wooden rear stair to the top floor, a bulkhead into the cellar and a gravel lot. The owner lives in Brooklyn. A sensible first phase fixes the front door's closer and strike, puts a call-to-phone entry panel on the front door so the dead in-unit stations no longer matter, and adds a keypad lock and light at the rear stair. Phase two adds contacts on the bulkhead and rear door, then two cameras covering the lot and the rear stair, on a recorder in a locked cabinet with a UPS. In a historic district, the panel's finish is checked with the commission first.

Frequently asked questions

If the Multiple Dwelling Law doesn't reach us, what legally requires a secure front door?

Three things: the state Property Maintenance Code, which wants locks on doors, windows and hatchways; the habitability warranty (Real Property Law §235-b), which extends to entrances and halls; and Multiple Residence Law §109 on lighting halls and stairs. Local code officers do the enforcing.

Does a Good Cause town require extra security equipment?

It does not add an equipment list. It governs renewals, evictions and rent increases where a locality opted in, which means tenants tend to stay longer and live with the same entry system for years. The habitability duty applies either way.

Does New York City's tenant data law cover a fob system in Yonkers or Newburgh?

Not directly; Local Law 63 of 2021 applies inside the five boroughs. Up here it still makes sense to settle with your attorney how long door logs are kept and who may open them, and to keep a key or code option so no tenant needs an app for their own door.

We live in the city and own a walk-up upstate. Do we need to be there for visits?

No. A tenant or a local handyman can let the technician in, or you can leave a lockbox code, and after each visit you get a written summary with photos. New work arrives as a priced plan you approve line by line.

What does the first visit cost?

Repairs are billed as a $250 service call: travel, tracing the fault between the entry and the apartment, and small fixes. Larger work gets a written quote before anything is done, and the $250 counts toward it.
