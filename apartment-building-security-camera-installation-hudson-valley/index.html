What New York law lets an owner record

Lobbies, hallways, stairs, laundry rooms, parking areas and building entrances are common areas where residents generally have no expectation of privacy, so owners can cover them. The hard lines are inside apartments and anywhere people undress. New York Penal Law 250.45 makes unlawful surveillance a felony, and the law presumes no legitimate purpose for a camera placed in a bathroom or bedroom. Hallway cameras should be angled along the corridor so they do not look into a unit when a door opens.

Audio is the second trap. New York's eavesdropping law, Penal Law 250.05, is built around consent to record a conversation, and a hallway camera that picks up voices sits in uncertain territory. Most owners simply switch audio off. Posting signs where cameras record and mentioning the system in leases is not always required, but it heads off disputes and costs almost nothing.

Rent-regulated buildings: why Kingston is different

The Emergency Tenant Protection Act applies in Kingston, where it survived a court challenge, and in a number of Westchester municipalities. Courts voided Newburgh's adoption in April 2024 and Poughkeepsie's in November 2024. In an ETPA building, tenants can file a state complaint for decreased building-wide services covering areas such as entrances, lobbies and halls. Whether your particular cameras are a listed service turns on how the building was registered, so ask your lawyer. The practical rule is simple: never pull out a working system without installing its replacement in the same visit.

County paperwork that can apply

  • Putnam: the county electricians' law (Chapter 145) licenses low-voltage and limited-data technicians and names video surveillance cabling, so the licence question in Brewster or Carmel is a real one.
  • Rockland: new 120-volt circuits for a recorder or camera pole fall under the county's electrical licensing (Chapter 250). Its rewritten home-improvement law (Chapter 286), effective May 14, 2026, reaches dwellings of three units or fewer, not large complexes.
  • Ulster: master electricians are licensed at county level under Chapter 185, which matters for any new power.
  • Orange and Dutchess: no county contractor licence turned up in their published guidance, but city and town building departments still enforce the state code, and the state's security installer licence applies everywhere.

Apartment building cameras in the Hudson Valley: FAQs

Can a landlord record tenants in hallways and lobbies?

Yes. Hallways, lobbies, stairs, laundry rooms and parking areas are common areas, and owners can cover them with video. Cameras must never go inside apartments, bathrooms or changing areas, and hallway cameras should not be aimed into units when doors open.

Should our building cameras record sound?

Usually not. New York’s eavesdropping law turns on consent to record conversations, and a hallway camera that picks up voices creates unnecessary legal risk. Video alone answers most package, door and vandalism questions.

How long should an apartment building keep its footage?

No New York rule sets a fixed period for private buildings, so choose one deliberately. Keep enough days to cover the delay before tenants report thefts or damage, and make sure the drive is sized to hold that period at the quality you need.

We own buildings in three counties. Can one camera standard cover all of them?

Yes, and it makes management easier. Use the same camera models, recorder, naming pattern and retention everywhere, record locally at each building, and add each county’s permits and licences as a separate checklist for that site.

Why do motion alerts at our suburban complex go off all night?

Deer, branches and insects drawn to infrared light trigger basic motion detection. Cameras that classify people and vehicles, detection zones that exclude the treeline and lawn edges, and a motion-activated white light at key entrances bring alerts down to the ones worth checking.
