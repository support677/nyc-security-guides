Apartment building security camera installation NYC projects that end badly almost always skipped the policy step: no one put in writing what the system is for, who gets to watch it, or how long video is kept, and the board ends up arguing about angles after the cable is already in the walls. This guide walks an owner, managing agent or co-op/condo board through those decisions in the order they actually come up. When you are ready for numbers, the apartment building security camera installation NYC page from Abstract Enterprises Security Systems shows its building price ranges.

Step 1: Write the camera policy before you collect quotes

A one-page policy prevents more friction than any camera spec. Have counsel review it, but the substance is yours to decide:

  • Purpose. Package theft, strangers following residents in, vandalism, slip-and-fall claims. Name the problems, because they decide where cameras go.
  • Areas covered. Common areas only: entrances, lobby, mailroom, hallways, stairs, elevators, garage, laundry and storage rooms. Never inside a unit.
  • Viewers. Who can watch live, who can review recordings, and who can export a clip. Usually that is the managing agent and the super, not every board member.
  • Retention. The number of days footage stays before it is overwritten.
  • Resident requests. How a resident asks for footage after a stolen package, and how quickly they get an answer.
  • Notice. Signs at the entrances stating that common areas are recorded.

Retention deserves a real number rather than whatever the recorder defaults to. Too short and the clip is gone before the resident notices the missing box; too long and the building is holding video it has no reason to keep. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems suggests 30 to 60 days for residential buildings. The figure you pick sets how much drive space the recorder needs, and the security camera storage calculator gives a quick estimate of days per drive for your camera count.

Step 2: Rank the zones, then count cameras

Few buildings cover everything in the first phase. Rank areas by how often they generate a complaint, an insurance claim or a police report:

PriorityZoneWhat the footage has to show
1Street door and vestibuleA clear face for everyone entering, including whoever slips in behind a resident
2Mailroom or package shelfHands on boxes, not just a figure somewhere in the room
3Lobby and elevator cabsThe path from the door to the upper floors
4Rear yard, service door, trash areaThe doors people forget to lock
5Garage gate and parkingVehicles coming and going, and plates where the gate is a problem
6Hallways and stairwellsFloor-by-floor movement; usually the costliest tier because of riser cabling

A small walk-up often stops after zone 4. A large elevator building usually works through the whole list, sometimes over two budget years.

Where the money goes

In a multi-story building the camera is the inexpensive part. The cost is in getting cable from a recorder in a locked closet up through the risers to each floor, sealing every fire-rated penetration along the way, and working through plaster, brick and occupied hallways. That is why Abstract Enterprises Security Systems prices building work per installed camera rather than per device. The ranges published on its page:

  • About $500 to $1,500 per camera, all-in.
  • A small walk-up covering the entry, lobby and mailroom with roughly 6 to 8 cameras: commonly $5,000 to $12,000.
  • A full elevator building with hallways, elevators and a garage at 16 to 32 cameras: around $10,000 to $25,000 and up.

Two decisions move the total most: how many floors receive hallway cameras, and where recording happens. A local network video recorder (NVR) carries no per-camera monthly charge. Cloud-managed platforms bill monthly per camera, which the Abstract Enterprises Security Systems page estimates at roughly $20 to $80, but they can make sense for a manager who oversees a portfolio of buildings from one screen.

How the building type shapes the job

BuildingWhat usually drives the install
Pre-war walk-upRarely a usable riser, so cable often runs in painted surface raceway up the stairwell. Short runs, modest camera counts.
Elevator buildingExisting shafts may have room for cable; cameras in the cab need coordination with the elevator maintenance company.
Co-op or condoWritten board approval, the building's alteration-hour rules, and an insurance certificate listing the corporation and managing agent as additional insured — all in hand before the first ladder goes up.
Garden or courtyard complexMany entrances spread across several buildings, outdoor runs, and long distances back to the recorder.
Mixed-use with stores belowKeep the residential system separate from the shop owner's cameras so each party controls its own footage.

What install week looks like

  1. Walkthrough. The installer walks each zone with the super and notes cable paths, power and a locked spot for the recorder.
  2. Written scope. Camera positions, models, recorder size and retention, cable routes and the documents the board needs. This is the packet the board approves.
  3. Paperwork and scheduling. The certificate of insurance goes to the managing agent; work hours and elevator padding are booked with the super.
  4. Cable first, then cameras. Runs go in floor by floor, then cameras are mounted and aimed against the zone list.
  5. Handover. App access only for the people named in the policy, a short lesson on exporting a clip, and a camera map for the building file.

Questions to put to any installer

  • Can you show your New York State security installer license and an insurance certificate that names our corporation and managing agent?
  • Exactly where will cable run, and how will fire-rated penetrations be sealed?
  • How many days will the quoted drives record at our camera count and resolution?
  • Is there any monthly fee, and who owns the recorder and the footage?
  • Can the system connect to our intercom or door access later without starting over?

Frequently asked questions

Is a NYC landlord required to install security cameras in the building?

Generally no. Cameras in a residential building are a decision for the owner or the board, not something city rules broadly require. Residents who want them, often after a run of package thefts, usually get further with a short written request that lists specific incidents than with a legal argument.

Can my landlord put a camera inside my apartment?

Not without your knowledge and consent. New York Penal Law 250.45 makes covert recording in a home a crime in many situations. Building cameras belong in common areas such as entrances, lobbies, hallways and mailrooms.

Can the building run its cameras on a tenant's internet connection?

It is a poor arrangement. A building system should record to the building's own recorder on the building's own internet service, so remote viewing does not depend on one resident's account or router.

Do co-op and condo boards need a licensed contractor for cameras?

Yes in practice. New York requires a state license to install security systems, and most proprietary leases and house rules require licensed, insured contractors who supply a certificate of insurance naming the building.

How long should an apartment building keep camera footage?

Pick a number and put it in the written policy. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems recommends 30 to 60 days for residential buildings, long enough for a resident to report a stolen package a week or two later.

Do residents have to be told the building has cameras?

Posting notice at the entrances is standard practice, costs nothing and heads off most complaints. Ask your building's attorney what applies to your specific property.
