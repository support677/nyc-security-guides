Fire Alarm Installation Long Island: How Nassau and Suffolk Approvals Actually Work

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-14

Fire alarm installation Long Island work is shaped less by the equipment than by who signs off on it. Nassau County has a county fire marshal; in Suffolk, each town runs its own fire prevention office with its own forms and inspection habits. This guide explains how that approval path works, what a homeowner needs versus what a business needs, and what to have ready so the job does not stall. The approval path for a commercial system, step by step

Every Long Island office handles this a little differently, but the sequence looks like this.

  1. Identify the authority. In Nassau, plan review goes through the county fire marshal. In Suffolk, it is the town: Brookhaven, Islip, Huntington, Babylon, Smithtown and the rest each have their own bureau. Incorporated villages can add their own building department on top.
  2. Design and drawings. Brookhaven's Division of Fire Prevention, for example, asks for a PDF of drawings stamped and signed by the design professional of record, at no smaller than 1/8-inch scale, with HVAC zones clearly shown, plus manufacturer cut sheets and battery calculations.
  3. Owner authorization and license. Brookhaven also wants a signed, notarized property owner authorization and a copy of the installer's New York State fire alarm license. Its submissions now go through the town's online portal only; paper is no longer accepted.
  4. Permit issued to the contractor. Huntington's fire prevention FAQ says the licensed company installing the equipment is the one that applies for the construction permit.
  5. Install and pre-test. Wiring, devices, panel programming, monitoring communicator, then a full test by the installer before the marshal arrives.
  6. Witnessed acceptance test. In Huntington, the contractor of record requests the inspection, and a fire marshal witnesses it. Plan for the occupancy or certificate of completion to wait on this visit.

The takeaway for a business owner: the contractor carries most of this, but you control two things that often cause delays — signing the owner authorization promptly and giving access for the test. Ask your installer early which town office is handling your address and what they will need from you.

What a Long Island house usually needs

Most homes here were built decades before interconnected detection was standard, and many have been expanded since. A useful walk-through starts with these spots:

  • The boiler or furnace room. Oil heat is common on the island, so smoke and CO detection near the burner and on every level matters.
  • Finished basements. If anyone sleeps down there, detectors belong inside the bedroom and just outside it. Picking a detector type suited to damp basements avoids the nuisance alarms that lead people to pull batteries.
  • Attached garages. A heat detector, rather than a smoke detector, fits a space with car exhaust and dust.
  • Raised and rebuilt South Shore houses. Post-storm reconstruction triggered current code, and the utility area under a raised house is often where detection was missed.
  • Kitchens. Photoelectric detectors placed away from the stove cut cooking false alarms.

If you rent out a unit, New York requires the owner to provide working smoke detectors in each dwelling unit and CO alarms near sleeping areas, so a two-family or an accessory apartment is part of the job, not an extra.

A short scenario: the inherited strip-mall panel

A tenant takes over a former deli on a Suffolk commercial strip and plans a small café. The old panel shows a trouble light and nobody knows the code to silence it. That one space might need a site survey to find out whether the existing devices and wiring are reusable, a new or reprogrammed panel, a monitoring account in the new tenant's name, kitchen-hood suppression tied into the panel, and a fresh submission to the town because the use is changing. None of that is unusual; it simply means the schedule should start with the fire alarm, not end with it.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. Which office is the authority for my address, and have you filed there before?
  2. Who prepares and stamps the drawings, and is that cost in the quote?
  3. Can any existing wiring or devices be reused, or does the panel brand force a replacement?
  4. Is monitoring included, and who holds the monitoring contract?
  5. Who schedules the acceptance test, and what do you need from me that day?
  6. What annual inspection and testing will the system need after it passes?

Who approves a fire alarm system on Long Island?

In Nassau County it is the county fire marshal. In Suffolk, each town's fire prevention office (Brookhaven, Islip, Huntington, Babylon and others) reviews plans and witnesses the acceptance test.

What does a Suffolk town need for a fire alarm permit?

Requirements vary by town. Brookhaven, for example, asks for a notarized owner authorization, stamped drawings at 1/8-inch scale or larger showing HVAC zones, device cut sheets, battery calculations and a copy of the installer's NYS fire alarm license, submitted online.

Can I install my own fire alarm system?

For a business, no: the permit goes to a licensed installer. A homeowner can install battery smoke alarms, but hardwired interconnected systems are best left to a licensed professional.

Does a landlord have to provide smoke detectors on Long Island?

Yes. Under New York law the owner must provide working smoke detectors in each dwelling unit and carbon monoxide alarms near sleeping areas.
