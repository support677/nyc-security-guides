Step 1: check the models before anything is mounted

Amcrest sells two quite different families under one logo. The PoE line (bullet, turret and dome cameras plus an Amcrest NVR) records locally and keeps working when your internet drops. The Wi-Fi and smart-home line is built around the app and the cloud. Mixing them is possible, but they are set up differently, so write down every model number on the boxes before you plan anything.

The model numbers matter for a second reason. Much of Amcrest's hardware has historically come from Dahua designs, and federal rules in 2026 tightened what Dahua-based equipment can be sold and used for in certain security settings. Amcrest itself is not on the FCC's Covered List, so for most homeowners this is a check, not a crisis. If the property is a business taking public money, a nonprofit with a federal grant, or anything near a government contract, confirm the specific models against the funder's terms before buying more of them.

Step 3: match the cable route to the house

Long Island houses fall into a handful of patterns, and each one has a natural path for camera cable.

Slab ranches

There is no basement, so every run goes up: from the recorder into the attic, across the joists and down inside a soffit to each camera. Attic work is best done outside the hottest summer weeks and before insulation is added.

Capes and split-levels

Knee walls and short stair runs between levels make some routes awkward. A good installer will often put the recorder on the middle level so no run has to climb two floors.

Colonials and raised ranches with basements

The basement ceiling becomes the main highway. Cables run along the joists to the rim, exit through a sealed hole and rise to the camera.

Waterfront and bayside houses

Salt air is hard on connectors. Every outdoor joint should sit inside a junction box, and drip loops matter more here than inland.

Step 4: install day, hour by hour

  1. Walk the property together. Stand where each camera will go and look at the live view on a phone or a test monitor before drilling.
  2. Bench-test everything. Every camera is plugged into the recorder on a table, updated and given a name first.
  3. Pull cable. Runs are labelled at both ends ("driveway", "garage east") so the next person knows what feeds what.
  4. Mount and seal. Junction boxes, silicone at every penetration, and cameras angled slightly down to keep the sky out of the frame.
  5. Configure the recorder. Fixed network addresses, recording schedules, motion zones that skip the street, and a changed admin password.
  6. Hand over. App access for each household member, a short lesson on finding and exporting a clip, and a written list of what was installed.

Questions to ask any installer

  • Will you test every camera on my recorder before mounting it?
  • Where will the recorder live, and is that spot cool, dry and on a battery backup?
  • How will cable reach the garage, and is that run rated for outdoor or underground use?
  • Will my recordings stay on my own recorder, with cloud as an option rather than a requirement?
  • Do you need landlord, HOA or village approval for any of the exterior work?
  • Who keeps the admin password, and will you write it down for me?

How it plays out on a typical Suffolk lot

Picture a 1970s colonial with an eight-camera Amcrest PoE kit. The basement makes six of the runs simple. The detached garage, roughly 140 feet back, is the real job: the kit cables are far too short, and a Wi-Fi camera placed there tends to drop out in the evenings. The usual fix is an outdoor-rated cable to a small PoE switch inside the garage, which then powers the cameras on that side. It adds a few hours to the day, and afterwards the garage cameras record like every other camera on the system.

Frequently asked questions

Are Amcrest cameras still allowed after the FCC's 2026 changes?

Amcrest as a brand is not on the FCC Covered List, but some of its hardware has historically been built on Dahua designs, and Dahua equipment is restricted for certain security and infrastructure uses. For a typical home the practical step is to check each model number before buying more and to keep firmware current. Sites using federal grant money should check NDAA terms with the funder first.

Can I install an Amcrest kit myself and just pay someone to run the cable?

Yes. Many owners buy the kit and hire out the part they cannot do easily: attic and basement runs, drilling through siding or brick, and sealing exterior penetrations. Ask the installer to bench-test every camera on the recorder before mounting, so a dead unit is found on the counter, not on the eave.

Will the cables that came in the box reach my detached garage?

Often not. Kit cables are sized for an average house, and a garage at the end of a Long Island driveway can need a much longer run. A single Ethernet segment can go up to about 100 metres (328 feet); beyond that, or between buildings, the installer adds a small PoE switch, an extender or a buried outdoor-rated run.

Should I use Amcrest Wi-Fi cameras or PoE cameras?

Use PoE wherever a cable can reach, because it carries power and video on one wire and does not depend on your router. Keep Wi-Fi models for spots where a cable truly cannot go, and test signal strength at the mounting point before you commit.
