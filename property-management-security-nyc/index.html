Property Management Security NYC work starts from a different place than an owner's single-building project: you answer for a list of addresses, several owner entities and a super who has to keep everything running between visits. This guide is for the managing agent who has just picked up buildings, or who is tired of paying a different vendor to rediscover the same cellar closet. It covers what to take control of first, how to set one standard across mixed buildings, the city rules that shape the work, and the published prices behind Property Management Security NYC service.

Week one on a new building: find out what you control

When an agent changes, the hardware usually stays and the passwords leave. Before approving any repair or upgrade, make a one-page inventory for each address. The column that matters is the last one.

One standard, many buildings

A portfolio rarely gets uniform hardware, and it doesn't need it. What it needs is a short written standard that every new job follows, so any technician can walk into any building and read the closet.

  • One credential format. Pick a fob type and stick with it, so a tenant transfer between two of your buildings doesn't mean new hardware.
  • One or two intercom families. An audio panel suits a small walk-up, video with phone calling suits larger buildings, and spare stations for each family can sit in one storeroom.
  • Labels and a map in every closet. The breaker for the intercom's power supply, the controller's location, and reset steps for a door that won't release.
  • Retention and clocks set on day one. A recorder with the wrong time or a full drive is the usual reason an incident clip can't be found.

Rules that decide how and when the work happens

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires a two-way intercom and self-locking entrance doors in class A buildings with eight-plus units, where construction or conversion came after January 1, 1968; older buildings must add one when most tenants put the request in writing. Housing Maintenance Code §27-2041.1 says unit entrance doors and hall doors must be self-closing and self-latching, and under Local Law 71 of 2022 HPD checks those doors on its own initiative in 300 class A buildings every year, allowing 14 days to fix a violation. In 2025, HPD violations mentioning an intercom ran 1,637 in the Bronx, 1,157 in Brooklyn, 1,020 in Manhattan, 342 in Queens and 24 on Staten Island.

Scheduling follows 28 RCNY §25-101: a week of written notice for repair or improvement work in an apartment, 24 hours before an inspection, and weekday hours of 9 to 5 unless the tenant agrees to another time. That matters most on intercom risers, where one bad pair can affect a whole vertical line and every apartment on it needs an appointment on the same day. The office sets those appointments; tenants should hear about the work only through notices the office approves.

Turnover, fobs and the tenant data law

With citywide rental vacancy at 1.4 percent in the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey, moves are fewer, but every one still means a fob to deactivate, a directory listing to update and an app login to shut down. If the office can't do that from one screen, each move-out turns into a service visit.

Smart-access buildings also carry Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Act. In plain terms:

  • Get a tenant's express consent before collecting their credential data, and publish a plain-language privacy policy.
  • Keep the system encrypted and its firmware current.
  • Never sell the data; tenants can sue, and the law sets damages between $200 and $1,000 for each sale.
  • Keep a non-smart way for every tenant to reach their own unit.
  • Delete or anonymize a former tenant's reference data within 90 days, which a well-configured system can do on a schedule.

None of this is legal advice; have counsel review the policy before a new system goes live.

Comparing bids across a portfolio

Two quotes for the same building can differ by thousands and both be honest, because they assume different things. Before choosing, check that each bid states:

  1. The same count of apartment stations, readers and doors.
  2. Whether existing wiring is reused, and what happens if a riser fails its test.
  3. The monthly platform fee per building, and who owns the software license.
  4. Removal of the old equipment.
  5. What you get at the end: labeled cables, a closet map, and admin logins in the owner's name.

Scenario: six walk-ups change agents in the fall

A manager takes over six small walk-ups in one borough. Three have audio intercoms with half the apartment stations disconnected, one has a cloud panel whose subscription lapsed, and two have recorders nobody can log into. The sensible order: recover logins and reactivate the cloud panel, repair the audio buildings in one grouped day, and replace recorders only after checking whether the cameras and cable can stay. Front-door closers and strikes get a check before the first freeze, since a door that caught in October may not catch in January, and the package area gets a delivery code before the holiday surge.

Frequently asked questions

Should we let tenants mount Ring-style doorbell cameras on hallway doors?

That is set by the lease and building rules, so management decides. If the office allows them, a building-owned camera in the hall usually serves everyone better: it covers the whole corridor, it is never aimed into another apartment, and footage stays with management instead of on a tenant's account.

Are common-area cameras allowed to see into apartments?

No camera should be aimed so it looks into an apartment through an open door or window. Point hallway cameras along the corridor, avoid recording audio unless counsel approves it, and set a retention period the office can explain to tenants.

Do we have to give tenants a key if the building switches to fobs or an app?

Under Local Law 63 of 2021, a tenant cannot be required to use the smart-access system to enter their own apartment. Keep a key or another non-app option for the unit door, and check the lease and policy with counsel.

How do we upgrade an old analog intercom in a small building without replacing everything?

Start by testing the riser wiring. If the pairs test clean, a new lobby panel can often reuse them, and some panels call tenants' phones so apartment stations become optional. If the wiring fails, compare re-pulling it with a system that skips in-unit wiring altogether.

What does a service call cost when we don't know what is wrong?

It is the $250 service call: the visit, testing the line between the lobby panel and the unit, and simple fixes. Anything bigger is priced before it is done.
