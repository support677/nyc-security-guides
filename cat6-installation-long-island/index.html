Cat6 installation Long Island work splits into two very different jobs: wiring a house that has an attic and a basement, and wiring a wide, single-story commercial building where the far desk is a long walk from the network closet. Both need the same cable, but the planning problems are not alike. This guide covers each, explains the one distance rule that catches people out, and lists what a good quote should include. Cat5e, Cat6 or Cat6A: a plain comparison

CableRated bandwidthGood forWhen to choose it
Cat5e100 MHzBasic gigabit linksMostly repairs and matching an existing system
Cat6250 MHzGigabit desks, Wi-Fi access points, PoE cameras, phonesThe sensible default for homes and most offices
Cat6A500 MHz10-gigabit at full lengthServer rooms, links between closets, or when the drawings call for it

Cat6 is the right answer for the large majority of Long Island jobs. The extra headroom over Cat5e tolerates a warm ceiling or a long run without the link slowing down, and the price gap is small enough that saving on it rarely makes sense for cable you will keep for a decade or more.

The distance rule: 90 metres is cable, not floor

Ethernet standards allow 90 metres (about 295 feet) of permanent cable from the closet to the wall jack, plus short patch cords at each end. The trap is that the run is measured along the cable's real path: up the wall, across the ceiling, around ducts and back down. A desk that looks 200 feet away on a floor plan can easily use more than the limit by the time the cable is in.

That matters on Long Island because commercial space is spread out horizontally. A flex building in Hauppauge, a medical office in Mineola or a campus building on the Route 110 corridor can be wide enough that one closet cannot reach every corner. Buying a higher category of cable does not stretch the limit. The fix is a second network closet, linked to the first with fiber, and it is far cheaper to plan that before cable is pulled than after it fails testing.

Wiring a Long Island house

Houses here are usually easier to wire than city apartments, because most have an accessible attic, basement or crawl space. A typical approach:

  • Pick one spot for the network hub — often the basement near where the internet service enters, or a utility closet. Every cable runs home to it.
  • Colonials and two-story homes are usually wired down from the attic to second-floor rooms and up from the basement to first-floor rooms.
  • Ranches are often the simplest: one attic or one basement can reach almost every room.
  • Split-levels take more thought because the floors overlap and the lower level may be finished with little access.
  • Older homes with plaster walls or finished basements take longer, and the installer should tell you that at the walkthrough, not halfway through the job.

One local caution: attic temperatures in a Long Island summer get high. If an attic run will power a camera or access point, solid copper cable in a modest bundle keeps it reliable through August. Homeowners adding PoE cameras can size storage for them with the free camera storage calculator.

Wiring a small business

Strip retail, restaurants, dental and medical offices and light industrial units each bring their own constraints. Point-of-sale terminals belong on wired connections. Kitchens and back rooms are hot and greasy, so cable routes and jacks need protection. Medical offices want a drop in every exam room and work done outside patient hours. Warehouses need cable tray instead of hooks, a lift for high work, and routes planned so the next change to the racking does not put cable in the way. In every case, work that has to happen overnight or on a weekend should be agreed and priced up front.

Five things to check in any cabling quote

  1. Solid copper, not CCA. Copper-clad aluminum is cheaper and runs hotter, which is a bad match for PoE devices.
  2. Every drop tested before ceilings or walls are closed, with results you can keep.
  3. Labels at both ends and a port map, so the next technician does not need a toner to find a room.
  4. Matching parts. Cat6 cable terminated on Cat5e jacks performs like Cat5e.
  5. A service loop of spare cable at each end, so a jack can be re-terminated or moved without a new pull.

Licensing and permits on the Island

Long Island does not have one simple answer to "is my installer licensed." Suffolk County licenses electrical trades at county level, some towns and villages in both counties set their own requirements, and Nassau adds its own consumer-affairs licensing. Security work such as cameras and access control is separately licensed by the state. Ask every bidder which license covers your exact scope and town, and confirm with your local building department whether the job needs a permit.

Frequently asked questions

What does a Cat6 drop cost on Long Island?

Abstract Enterprises Security Systems lists a Cat6 drop on a run up to 100 feet at $296, including cable, jack, plate, termination and testing. Longer runs are measured on site, and racks, switches and conduit are quoted separately.

Can Ethernet be run in a finished house without opening walls?

Usually, yes. Most Long Island homes have an attic or basement, so cable can be fished down or up interior walls with small openings at the jack. Finished basements and plaster walls take longer.

Should I install Cat6 or Cat6A?

Cat6 handles gigabit desks, Wi-Fi access points and PoE cameras in most homes and offices. Cat6A makes sense for 10-gigabit links at full length, server rooms or links between network closets.

What is the maximum length of a Cat6 run?

About 90 metres, or 295 feet, of permanent cable from closet to jack, measured along the actual cable path. Wide buildings that exceed it need a second closet linked with fiber.

What is CCA cable and why avoid it?

CCA is copper-clad aluminum. It is cheaper but has higher resistance and runs hotter, which hurts PoE devices and long runs. Ask for solid copper cable.

Do I need a permit to install network cable on Long Island?

It depends on the town or village, the building and the scope. Check with the local building department and ask your installer which license covers the work.
