Lorex Camera Installation Brooklyn goes best when you start from the doors rather than the cameras. A Brooklyn row house is long and narrow with two exposed ends: the stoop, the areaway and the cellar door under the stoop at the street, and a garden door onto a fenced yard at the back. Decide which of those you need to see, then where the recorder can live, then whether every camera can be wired. The price follows from those three answers, and so does whether the video is any use on the day you need it.

Where the recorder lives decides most of the labor

In most row houses the recorder belongs in the cellar: the joists are open, cables can run front to back along them, and the stoop and garden door are both a short climb away. Two things go wrong down there. Cellars are damp, so the recorder should sit off the floor, away from the water heater and the dehumidifier drain, with room to breathe. And it often ends up on the same power strip as the boiler controls, where someone unplugs it by accident; give it its own outlet, ideally behind a small battery backup.

If the cellar ceiling has been finished and sealed, the easy routes disappear and some cameras may move to the concealed-cable rate. If the internet comes in on the parlor floor, that is not a problem: one network cable down to the cellar connects the recorder to the router.

Owners on Lorex forums describe Fusion Wi-Fi cameras that show "offline" after a power cut until they are restarted, or that vanish every day or so until the recorder is rebooted. In a brick house the cure is usually the same: wire every camera that can be wired, and keep Wi-Fi for the one spot that truly cannot take a cable.

Neighbors, tenants and the yard

Brooklyn yards are boxed in by other people's windows. Under New York Civil Rights Law §52-a, filming the yard next door without the neighbor's written permission exposes you to a lawsuit, so yard cameras should look at your own ground, with privacy masks drawn over the neighbors' yards in the Lorex settings.

In a two- or three-family house, settle the access question before setup: the recorder stays with the landlord, in their own apartment or a locked cellar room, tenants get live view of shared areas such as the street door, the alley and the bins, and nobody gets a camera pointed at another household's door or windows. When a tenant moves out, delete their app access that day.

Sizing the drive: days of recording

It depends on the bitrate of each camera, how many cameras record and whether they run continuously or only when something moves. A 4K camera aimed at a busy street pushes far more data than one aimed at a quiet yard. The recorder can show each channel's bitrate, and Lorex publishes its own hard drive calculator; you can also run the numbers in this security camera storage calculator. Installed systems here ship with a drive holding at least two weeks, and a bigger drive is the cheapest upgrade if you want a month.

When the app says the recorder is offline

  1. Check whether the cameras still show on a monitor plugged into the recorder. If they do, recording is fine and the problem is the internet path.
  2. Restart in order: modem first, then router, then the recorder, a minute or two apart.
  3. If the internet provider swapped the modem or you installed new mesh Wi-Fi, the recorder may have a new address or no route out; that is a settings fix, not a hardware one.
  4. Resist deleting the recorder from the app and re-adding it unless you know the device password; owners report that a restart alone often brings it back.
  5. Gaps in the recording timeline, or a drive the recorder reports as abnormal, point to a failing hard drive, which is a straightforward replacement.

Getting a clip to the police

After recent Brooklyn break-ins, NYPD investigators have asked the public for time-stamped doorbell and camera video. Three habits make yours usable: keep the recorder's clock set correctly (check it after every power cut), name each camera by location so a clip makes sense to a stranger, and know how to export a time window to a USB stick from the recorder itself rather than screen-recording your phone.

Frequently asked questions

What do Brooklyn Lorex packages cost?

The published Brooklyn packages begin at $1,080 for a two-camera system and $1,679.99 for four 4K cameras with the recorder, which covers cable runs to 100 feet each, two weeks or more of storage and setting up the app. Six- and eight-camera Fusion systems start at $2,274 and $2,950. Sales tax is extra and the written quote after the walk-through is final.

I bought a Lorex kit at a club store. Can it be installed for me?

Yes. Installing your own Lorex cameras and recorder is published at $175 per camera line, which pays for the mounting, cable runs to 100 feet and wiring each camera into your recorder. Concealed in-wall runs are $200 per camera, and other add-ons are priced before work starts.

What is the best spot for the recorder in a brownstone?

Usually in the cellar, raised off the floor, on its own outlet and away from the water heater and dehumidifier, because the open joists give short cable routes to the stoop and the garden door. If the cellar ceiling is sealed or the house has no cellar, a ventilated closet works, with longer cable runs.

Can my Lorex camera see into my neighbor's yard?

Aim it at your own property. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives neighbors a claim if your camera records their backyard without their written consent. Lorex recorders let you draw privacy masks over areas you should not record.

Why do my Lorex Wi-Fi cameras keep going offline?

In brick row houses the usual causes are weak Wi-Fi through masonry walls and cameras that do not reconnect after a power cut or router change. Restarting in order (modem, router, recorder) often restores them, but the lasting fix is to wire any camera that can take a cable.
