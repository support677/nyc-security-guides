Start with the question the video has to answer

Before anyone talks megapixels, write one sentence per lot: "If something happens here, I need to know ___." On a commuter lot the blank is usually "which car pulled in beside mine and when," because nobody notices the damage until the evening walk back from the train. On a plaza it is often "who was behind the building after close." At a winery or pick-your-own farm it might be "where the fender-bender in the grass overflow happened and which way the other driver left." That sentence decides whether you need wide overview cameras, tighter identification views at the entrance, or both.

The expensive part is usually the ground

Downstate, a trench through a landscape island can be an afternoon. Here, ledge and shallow bedrock show up in Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster sites often enough that a good installer asks about past excavation before quoting. Frost is the other half: a camera pole footing has to go below the frost line, and that line sits deeper in the northern counties than near the Sound. Neither problem is a reason to give up on a far corner. It is a reason to compare routes, such as surface conduit along a curb, a short fiber or Cat6 run to a pole that already has a base, a point-to-point wireless link that hops over the problem, or a solar camera where nothing should be dug at all.

Ask every bidder what they expect underground and what happens to the schedule if the trench meets rock. A vague answer now becomes a change order later.

Light poles, photocells and who owns the pole

Mounting cameras on existing light poles looks like free power, and sometimes it is. Two checks come first. The first is ownership: depending on where the property sits, a pole may belong to the owner, the town or village, or the utility, and utility poles have their own attachment process. The second is the circuit. Plenty of lot fixtures only get power when a dusk sensor or a clock tells them to, which leaves the pole without juice through daylight hours, and a camera that only records at night is not much use. Installers on forums describe the same fix again and again: a constant-power feed or a dedicated low-voltage run, not a camera spliced onto a switched lighting circuit. Pole circuits can also carry higher voltages than a typical building circuit, which is electrician territory, so expect a split scope where that applies.

Plan for February nights, not June afternoons

A lot that looks easy at noon in summer is a different job in winter. Snow gets plowed into piles that block low views. Leaves come off the trees and open sightlines that were hidden in August, then grow back. Rural lots on a state route may have almost no ambient light once the owner's fixtures cut back after hours, so ask to see a sample frame at night, not just a datasheet. Sometimes the honest recommendation is better lighting first. Deer, headlight sweep and blowing snow will also trigger plain motion alerts, which is why person and vehicle detection with zones and schedules needs a week of tuning after go-live.

A season-aware schedule

  1. Survey, day and night. Measure distances, note pole bases, check light levels after dark and talk through who approves the work.
  2. Itemized proposal. Each camera, each pathway and the recorder and storage behind them on separate lines, so two bids can be compared.
  3. Ground work while the ground is workable. Trenching frozen soil is slow, so underground portions are timed for it, or a far position moves to wireless or solar.
  4. Mount, aim and record. Cameras go up, views are checked against your one-sentence goals and retention is set.
  5. Tune and hand over. Alert zones and schedules get adjusted on real traffic, and you get remote access and a map of every camera.

Questions to ask any lot installer

  • Which positions are building-mounted and which need a pole, and why?
  • Is the pole power constant, and who confirmed it?
  • How many days of footage will the recorder keep at the settings you propose? (Our storage calculator lets you check the math.)
  • What surge protection and grounding goes on outdoor runs?
  • Who besides me has to sign off: a landlord, an HOA, a village board?

Parking lot camera questions from Valley owners

Can cameras run off our parking lot light poles?

Sometimes. The pole must have constant power, not a circuit switched off during the day by a photocell or timer, and you need to know who owns it. Where the lighting circuit is unsuitable, a dedicated low-voltage run or a solar camera is the usual answer.

What happens if the trench hits rock?

The route changes rather than the plan collapsing. Options include surface conduit along a curb, using an existing pole base, a point-to-point wireless link or a solar-powered position. A good proposal says up front how a rock surprise would be handled.

Will deer and headlights set off constant alerts?

On plain motion detection, yes. Person and vehicle detection with drawn zones and after-hours schedules cuts most of that, and the rules should be tuned after a week of live traffic.

Why do commuter lots need longer footage retention?

Damage to a car parked all day often is not noticed until evening or even days later, so the recording has to still exist when the owner finally reports it.

Can you cover a grass overflow lot that is only used in the fall?

Yes. A common approach is a permanent core of cameras plus temporary or solar positions on the overflow area during the busy season, recording to the same system.
