Start with the published per-drop rates

A "drop" is one cable from the patch panel or switch location to one jack at the outlet. Brooklyn is the company's base rate because its office is on Troy Avenue. Each figure below assumes a run no longer than 100 feet and already folds in the cable, faceplate, jack, termination on each side and a pass/fail test.

A worked estimate: a three-person studio in a converted building

Say a small design studio takes a floor in a former industrial building in Gowanus. Three desks at two drops each is six. Two wireless access points, because brick interior walls swallow signal. One printer. Two IP cameras, one at the door and one on the equipment. That is eleven Cat6 drops: 11 × $247 = $2,717 before anything else.

The "anything else" is real and is quoted on separate lines: the rack or wall cabinet, patch panel, switch, patch cords, conduit or tray where the ceiling is open, fire-stopping where cable passes through a rated wall, core drilling, lift rental for high ceilings, and evening or weekend work. In a space with an exposed deck and no closet, those lines can rival the cable itself. That is why the walkthrough matters more than the drop count.

For most desks, cameras and access points, Cat6 is the sensible default. Cat6A earns its premium on switch uplinks, server links and anywhere a written spec calls for 10 Gb over a long run. It does not stretch the 100-meter limit; past that distance the answer is fiber or a second closet.

The row-house home office

A common Brooklyn request is not an office at all: a brownstone or row house where someone works from the top floor and the internet comes in at the cellar. A forum thread from a new row-house owner sums up the problem: no attic, a finished basement and joists running the wrong way. The usual answer is to keep the long horizontal travel in the cellar, then come up once inside a closet stack, a chase beside the old gas line or behind the stair, and branch at each floor. One hardwired access point per floor beats a single powerful router, because the brick party walls and plaster stop signal between rooms. If the walls are lime plaster, the route should change before the plaster gets forced.

Renting a loft or storefront? Ask the landlord three things first

  • Who owns the cable at the end of the lease? Some leases make it a building improvement; others require the tenant to remove it.
  • What is overhead? An open deck, a tin ceiling or a drop ceiling change the price and the look of the job.
  • When can work happen? Mixed-use buildings with apartments above often limit noisy work to set hours.

Old dead cable from the previous tenant is common above Brooklyn ceilings. Removing it is a separate line, but leaving it makes the next move harder. For the bigger picture of racks, backbone and building coordination, see the Brooklyn structured cabling guide.

How to tell the job was done right

  • A test result for every run, not a few samples.
  • The same label on the jack and on the matching patch-panel port.
  • Solid copper conductors. Copper-clad aluminum (CCA) cable is cheaper per box but runs hotter under PoE and breaks more easily.
  • Cable supported on hooks or tray, not lying on ceiling tiles or stapled to joists.
  • A service loop left at the panel and the outlet, so a damaged jack can be redone without pulling new cable.
  • Jacks and panels rated to the same category as the cable.

Frequently asked questions

How much does one Cat6 drop cost in Brooklyn?

The published Brooklyn rate from Abstract Enterprises Security Systems is $247 per Cat6 drop on a run under 100 feet, with cable, jack, plate, terminations and testing included. Racks, switches, conduit and after-hours work are quoted separately.

Can you run Ethernet through a brownstone without opening the walls?

Often, by keeping horizontal runs in the cellar and rising through closets, old chases or the space behind the stair. Where there is no usable cavity, a short painted raceway is used instead of breaking lime plaster.

Is Cat6A worth the extra $106 a drop?

Only where 10 Gb at full distance is actually required, such as switch uplinks or server links. For desks, cameras and access points, Cat6 is the usual choice and costs $106 less per drop on the published Brooklyn list.

Is copper-clad aluminum cable okay for a small office?

It is best avoided. CCA has higher resistance, runs warmer when it carries power to cameras and access points, and its conductors break more easily. Solid copper is the standard for permanent runs.

Can my old phone wiring be reused for Ethernet?

Generally no. Old phone cable is usually a lower category and often spliced, so it cannot carry modern network speeds reliably. The old route can still show where new cable can travel.

What happens if a run is longer than 100 feet?

It is measured on site and priced against the actual pathway. If a run approaches the 100-meter limit for copper, a second closet or a fiber link is the better design.
