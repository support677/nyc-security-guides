Apartment Building Security Camera Installation Queens starts with a walk, not a camera count. Queens buildings leak at the edges: the twelve stairway entrances of a Jackson Heights garden co-op, the cellar laundry and rear lane of a six-story Forest Hills elevator house, the parking courts of a post-war campus in Glen Oaks, the garage ramp of a Long Island City rental tower, the side gate of a Woodside two-family. Local news reports keep describing the same sequence in Astoria, Elmhurst and Jackson Heights: a street door forced or picked, then the mailroom emptied. Follow that route through your own building and you have the outline of a camera plan. Pricing and the quote form are on the Apartment Building Security Camera Installation Queens page.

The walk, stop by stop

Take a notepad and go through the building the way someone who does not live there would. At each stop, write down what a camera there has to prove.

How each Queens building type changes the install

Garden co-ops in Jackson Heights, Sunnyside Gardens and Kew Gardens

These low brick buildings have a long shared cellar under several entrances, and that cellar is the natural cable route. Equipment usually lives in one small spot beside the boiler room or the super's workbench, with runs fanning out to each stairway door. Inside the Jackson Heights Historic District, and on landmarked Sunnyside Gardens buildings, anything facing the street needs a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit, so street cameras stay small, few and finished to match the masonry.

Six-story elevator houses along Queens Boulevard

Think lobby, elevator landing, mailroom, cellar laundry and back lane. The rack goes in the cellar or a closet off the lobby, and risers ride inside an old chase where one exists, otherwise in painted surface raceway up the stairwell. Elevator cab cameras are easiest when they are added during an elevator modernization, with the elevator contractor on site.

Post-war campuses and towers in the park

When there are dozens of buildings, each one gets its own switch, and the buildings are linked by fiber through existing conduit or by point-to-point radios between roofs, so copper never runs from building to building. Paths, play areas and parking courts matter as much as lobbies here.

New rental towers in Long Island City and Astoria

Many come with a developer-installed system that nobody on staff can log into. The first job is recovering admin access and auditing what records, before adding anything.

Two- and three-family houses

The owner usually lives downstairs and decides alone. Cover the front door, side gate, driveway and mailbox, and use privacy masking so no camera looks into the tenant's windows or private entry.

Notices people can read

Census figures show most Queens residents over age five use a language besides English at home. A sign printed only in English will go unread by a large share of the people walking past it. Post it in the languages your building actually speaks, at every entrance and on the laundry and cellar doors.

Reading the price list

The Queens page publishes building packages starting at $5,250 when a building needs four to eight cameras and climbing past $47,250 on big multi-building campuses. Spread across the cameras, that is about $525 to $1,575 each, labor included. The recorder is local, there is no monthly fee, and the footage belongs to the building. Service visits after installation, including camera adjustments made later, are billed at the published service rate of $195 per hour, three hours minimum, so it pays to settle camera angles during the install. Arrival windows are two hours, seven days a week until 8 PM.

The morning-after test

A system is only useful if someone can hand a clip to the police the day after a theft. Before the crew leaves, have the super or managing agent do this once, without help: find yesterday's 2 AM at the street door, export 60 seconds, and save it to a flash drive. Then agree who is allowed to view and export footage, how long it is kept, and who gets an alert if the recorder stops writing. Finding out after a break-in that the recorder died weeks ago is one of the most common tenant complaints online.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tenant put a camera in the lobby to catch a package thief?

Not without the landlord or board agreeing. The lobby is a common area, so anything mounted there needs permission. A tenant can raise the problem in writing and ask management to add or repair building cameras.

My landlord says the building cameras are down. What can I do?

Report the theft to the police and ask management in writing when the system will be repaired. A recorder that has quietly stopped is one of the most common faults; a service visit usually finds a failed drive, a dead power supply or a camera that dropped off the network.

Will management show me footage of my stolen package?

Building owners are not generally required to show tenants the footage, and many boards limit who can view it. The usual route is a police report; officers can request the clip from management, which is why a written footage policy and a working export process matter.

Do laundry room and hallway cameras record sound?

They should not. New York’s eavesdropping law makes it illegal to record conversations you are not part of, so common-area cameras are normally set to video only, with a posted notice.

Do I need permission to put cameras on a building in the Jackson Heights Historic District?

Yes. Street-facing work on buildings in a New York City historic district needs a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit, and the commission limits how many cameras can face the street and how much conduit can show.

What does a full building system cost in Queens?

On the published Queens list, prices begin at $5,250 for a building needing four to eight cameras and goes past $47,250 for big campuses. Per camera, installed, that is about $525 to $1,575, with a local recorder and no monthly fee.
