Elevator Access Control Installation Long Island projects tend to go wrong in the same place: a board or owner chooses a reader before deciding who should reach which floor, and when. The hardware is the easy part. The planning decides everything else, including which landings are restricted, during which hours, for which groups of people, and what the car does in an emergency. That plan sets how many readers and floor outputs the job needs and how much the building's elevator company has to do. This guide is about the planning a building should finish first.

Build the floor matrix first

Take a sheet of paper and give every landing a row. For each one, write who should be able to ride there and whether that changes by time of day. Here is an example for a six-story Nassau rental with a parking level and a roof deck:

LandingWho rides thereRestriction
P1 parkingResidents with a space, the superAlways; reader at the landing so the car will not answer a call from the garage without a credential
LobbyEveryoneNone
Floors 2 to 5Residents of that floor, staffAlways for anyone else
6, roof deck and gymAll residentsOpen 7 a.m. to 10 p.m., closed overnight

Every row with a restriction becomes a floor output on the controller side. Every landing with a reader becomes its own line on the quote. Once the board agrees on the matrix, the equipment count mostly writes itself, and nobody is surprised when the price arrives.

In-car reader or hall call reader?

Reader locationWhat it controlsTypical use
Inside the cabWhich floor buttons work after a tap or codeResidential floors, tenant floors in offices
At a landing's call buttonWhether the car answers that landing at allGarages, basements and side doors where someone could walk in from outside
BothEntry to the car and the destinationBuildings with an open garage plus private residential floors

Whatever you choose, treat every car in a bank the same way. If one car has a reader and its neighbor does not, people simply wait for the unrestricted car, and the money spent on the first one buys very little.

Three Long Island buildings, three different scopes

A South Shore co-op near the water

Barrier-island and canal-front buildings learned during Sandy that anything installed at lobby or garage level can be lost to a single surge of seawater. Here the access controller and its battery backup belong above the flood line, the garage landing usually gets a hall call reader, and the board should decide in writing how the cars behave if the access system loses power.

A Nassau medical office building

Along the Lake Success, New Hyde Park and Mineola corridors, the public changes all day: patients, families, couriers. What works is a single access system running a day schedule and a night schedule. While the practice is open the buttons behave normally except for staff-only floors; after closing, every floor needs a valid credential. Scheduled work windows matter too, because taking a car out of service in the middle of clinic hours is not an option.

A Suffolk senior residence with one hydraulic car

Priorities shift toward ease of use. Readers go at a comfortable height with clear feedback, and the credential is often a card or a wristband rather than a phone app. With a single car, the job is small in equipment terms but needs careful scheduling, because residents have no second elevator while the car is down.

Two contractors, two invoices

New York's elevator licensing law, Labor Law Article 33, keeps work on elevator equipment in the hands of licensed elevator contractors and mechanics. In practice that splits the job. The access contractor supplies and installs the readers, floor-release relays, wiring, credentials and programming. The elevator company makes the connection inside its controller, and the building books and pays for that visit directly. If remote management needs a network connection or fresh cable to the car, that is priced as its own line after the survey.

Get both scopes in writing and schedule the two crews together. Installers on industry forums describe the familiar standoff when floors stop restricting: the elevator company blames the access system and the access contractor blames the controller. A written acceptance test, plus the access system's event log showing whether a credential was granted at the moment of the ride, settles that argument in minutes instead of weeks.

Firefighters' operation comes first

ASME A17.1, the safety code New York uses for elevators, defines Phase I emergency recall, which sends the cars to a designated floor and ignores ordinary calls, and Phase II, which lets firefighters run a car from inside with a key. An access system must never stand in the way of either. It is designed to drop out when recall begins, and that behavior is tested with the elevator contractor at acceptance and recorded. Decide at the same meeting what the cars should do if the access system itself fails, and have the elevator contractor confirm the controller can do it.

Before you call: is it the fob or the system?

  • One person refused, everyone else riding: the credential has likely expired or been cancelled. The property manager can usually fix it in the software.
  • Everyone refused at one floor: check whether a schedule changed or that floor's output failed.
  • A whole car ignoring the readers: look at power and network to the access controller, then call both contractors.

FAQ

Who pays the elevator company for the controller connection?

The building arranges and pays for the elevator contractor's visit directly. The access contractor's price covers the readers, relays, wiring, credentials and programming, and lists the elevator company's scope separately.

Can our existing door fobs work in the elevator?

Often, yes. Many current readers read several common card formats, and a sample read of the fobs residents already carry is part of the survey, so you know before anything is ordered.

What happens to the readers when the fire department takes over the elevator?

They drop out. Phase I recall and Phase II firefighter operation override the access system, and that behavior is tested with the elevator contractor before the job is signed off.

Do we still need lobby access control if we restrict the elevator?

Usually yes. The front door controls who gets into the building; elevator restriction controls where they can go once inside, and the two work best together.

How much does elevator access control cost on Long Island?

Starting prices per car are $1,725 (Nassau) and $1,800 (Suffolk) for a card, fob or code reader, and $2,875 (Nassau) and $3,000 (Suffolk) for biometrics. A plan that limits each floor separately gets its own quote after the survey.
