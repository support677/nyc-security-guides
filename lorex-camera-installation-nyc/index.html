Lorex Camera Installation NYC: Store Kit or Wired NVR System? Sort Out What You Bought Before Anyone Drills

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-10

Lorex camera installation NYC requests usually start with a box that is already in the apartment, the store stockroom or the basement. Someone picked up a Lorex kit at a big-box store, opened it, and found that the cameras need cable pulled through brick, a recorder needs a home, and the app wants an account before anything shows up. This guide helps you figure out which kind of Lorex system you actually have, what the installation really involves in a New York building, and how to judge a quote. Step one: identify the Lorex family in the box

Lorex sells very different products under one logo, and they install in very different ways. Look at the label on the recorder (if there is one) and at the back of each camera.

Some Lorex Fusion recorders can also take selected Wi-Fi models alongside wired ones, so one box can hold footage from both. Even so, the wired cameras are the ones that keep recording when the router hiccups.

Why the wired version is worth the extra effort here

A PoE camera gets power and data over one cable, so there is no outlet to find on a brownstone facade or above a roll-down gate. The recorder keeps footage locally, which means no cloud plan is required to review last Tuesday, and the cameras keep recording during an internet outage. Wi-Fi cameras have their place, mostly indoors or where a cable truly cannot go, but a camera on the far side of a 14-inch brick wall from the router tends to drop out at exactly the wrong moment.

Where NYC buildings make a Lorex install harder

  • Cable distance. A standard Ethernet run tops out around 100 metres (328 feet). A rear-yard camera on a deep lot, or a roof camera in a five-story walk-up with the recorder in the cellar, can get close. A PoE switch closer to the cameras solves it.
  • PoE power budget. Lorex's own help pages warn that if the recorder's total PoE budget is exceeded, cameras drop or the system shuts down. PTZ and spotlight cameras draw more than small turrets, so the budget has to be added up before cameras are chosen.
  • Masonry and plaster. Coring brick for an exterior run, fishing cable inside plaster-and-lath walls, and sealing every penetration take far longer than screwing a camera to siding.
  • Permission. Renters need the landlord's written OK; co-op and condo owners usually need the board or managing agent to sign off on anything outside the unit, including hallway cable runs.
  • The recorder's home. It needs ventilation, power and ideally a battery backup. A sealed closet next to a boiler shortens hard-drive life.

What happens on install day

  1. Walk the views. Each camera gets a job: identify faces at the door, watch the package spot, cover the back gate, or catch plates at a driveway.
  2. Confirm routes and power. The tech measures each run, checks the PoE budget and decides whether a separate switch is needed.
  3. Pull, terminate and label cable. Labels at both ends save hours on every future service call.
  4. Mount and aim. Height and angle are set to avoid streetlight glare and to keep neighbors' windows out of frame.
  5. Configure the NVR. Set the time sync, recording schedule, person and vehicle detection zones, and storage overwrite. Then change the default credentials.
  6. Hand over the app. The Lorex Home app is set up on the owner's phone, separate logins are created for anyone else, and someone practices exporting a clip before the tech leaves.

What it costs

The Abstract Enterprises Security Systems Lorex page shows installed pricing, including a four-camera system with recorder from $1,899, plus a builder that prices each camera model, extra storage and hidden in-wall wiring. What moves a real quote is mostly the building: run length, brick versus drywall, whether cable can be concealed, and how much retention you want. A recorder in the same room as the cameras and four short runs is a very different job from a walk-up with the NVR three floors below.

Questions to ask any Lorex installer

  • Will every camera be wired, and if not, which ones are Wi-Fi and why?
  • What is the recorder's total PoE budget, and what will this camera mix draw?
  • How many days of recording will the included drive hold at the resolution you are setting?
  • Will default passwords be changed, and will I get my own admin login?
  • Who handles the landlord or board paperwork, and what proof of insurance can you provide?
  • If a camera goes offline later, is that a service call, and what does it cost?

A short scenario

Picture a two-family house in Queens with a four-camera Lorex PoE kit still in its carton. The owner wants the driveway, the front stoop, the side alley and the back yard covered. The recorder goes in the finished basement next to the router. Three runs drop down an exterior corner inside a painted raceway, and the fourth enters through the rim joist. The only surprise is the alley camera. It faces a neighbor's bedroom window, so it gets re-aimed and a privacy mask is drawn over the window before handover.

FAQ

Can I install a Lorex camera system myself in NYC?

For one or two cameras on your own house, often yes. Multi-camera wired systems in brick or pre-war buildings, anything in a rental or co-op, and storefront systems usually go better with a licensed installer who can route cable, size storage and handle building approval.

Do Lorex cameras need a subscription?

Not for wired NVR systems. Footage records locally on the recorder and the Lorex Home app shows it remotely without a monthly fee. Cloud storage is optional.

Will Lorex cameras keep recording if the internet goes down?

Wired cameras on an NVR keep recording to the local drive. You lose remote viewing on your phone until the connection returns.

Why do some of my Lorex cameras keep dropping offline?

Common causes are an exceeded PoE power budget, a cable run that is too long or poorly terminated, or a failing hard drive. Checking power draw and testing each run usually finds it.

Is Lorex allowed for buildings in NYC?

For private homes, rentals, co-ops and businesses, yes. The Abstract Enterprises Security Systems page notes Lorex is not NDAA-compliant for federal government or federally funded projects, so those sites should use a compliant brand.

The Abstract Enterprises Security Systems page lists a four-camera Lorex system with recorder installed from $1,899, with a builder for other sizes. The building, the cable routes and the storage you choose set the final number after a site visit.
