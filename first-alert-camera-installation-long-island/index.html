First Alert Camera Installation Long Island jobs almost always happen on a detached house: Census figures put roughly three in four Nassau homes and four in five Suffolk homes in single-family detached buildings. That means the cameras watch a driveway, a side gate, a back door and basement windows rather than a lobby, and each one has to get power and a usable signal through whatever the house is made of. First Alert's VX line from Resideo adds two Long Island wrinkles: the gear is sold through independent dealers who often hold your account, and many cameras end up tied to a monitored panel that falls under a county alarm permit. This guide covers both, plus how the house type sets the cable route. Installed prices are on the First Alert Camera Installation Long Island page.

Start with one question: who holds the account?

First Alert Professional is sold through authorized dealers who run their own businesses, and prices and contract terms differ from one dealer to the next. If you already have a ProSeries touchscreen or an older VISTA keypad, the cameras usually enroll through the dealer account that monitors it. Before anyone books a visit, check the keypad for a model label and hunt for the dealer's sticker, which is often on the metal control cabinet in a utility room or basement. That tells you whether the cameras will live under your existing monitoring plan, or whether a new account and video plan are needed.

The VX3 outdoor camera and VX5 indoor camera show up in the Total Connect app, and can also be viewed on ProSeries PROA7PLUS and PROA7PLUSC touchscreens once their firmware is at 03 or newer. Alarm Grid's notes on the VX3 list a Platinum plan or a stand-alone video plan as the service level cameras need, so ask what plan you are on before you buy hardware.

The signal walk, wall by wall

Resideo's VX3 install guide calls for a Wi-Fi reading of at least -65 dBm and about 2 Mbps of steady upload at the camera position. A router in a front living room often meets that at the front door and misses it at the back patio. So the first hour on site is spent walking, not drilling:

  1. Stand at each planned camera spot with a phone on the same Wi-Fi and run a speed test and a signal reading.
  2. Mark every spot that passes, every spot that is close, and every spot that fails.
  3. For a close spot, try moving the camera a few feet or adding a wired access point.
  4. For a failing spot, plan a PoE cable, because no setting in the app will fix a weak signal.
  5. Only then agree the final layout and power path for each camera.

Cameras, the panel and your alarm permit

Nassau and Suffolk run their alarm rules separately. Nassau's police department says every alarm system needs a permit and that a location can be suspended or moved to no police response once a location racks up a third false alarm within one calendar year; Suffolk has its own Alarm Management Program. A camera on its own is not an alarm, but once VX cameras are linked to a monitored panel, sloppy motion zones can turn into dispatch calls. Two habits help: draw detection zones that skip the street and the neighbor's yard, and keep camera clips as a way to check an alarm, not as a trigger that sends police on their own.

New York's General Business Law also requires a state license to install or service security alarm systems, while passive devices are treated differently. When cameras tie into a panel, ask the installer which license covers the work.

A typical case: the far end of a split-level

Picture a South Shore high ranch with a VX doorbell that works fine and a VX3 over the back slider that drops offline most evenings. The router sits in the upstairs living room, two half-levels and a kitchen away. A signal walk usually shows that back spot hovering near the minimum, and worse once everyone starts streaming after dinner. Moving the router rarely helps the front of the house. The cleaner fix is one PoE run from a small switch in the lower-level utility room, through a joist bay and out under the deck to the back camera, which takes Wi-Fi out of the equation for that position.

Questions to ask any First Alert installer

  • Will the cameras go on my existing dealer account, or a new one?
  • Which video plan do they need, and what does it cost each month?
  • Will you test signal at every spot before mounting?
  • Which cameras get PoE, and where will the switch live?
  • How will you set zones so the panel does not cause false dispatches?
  • Are equipment and labor shown as separate lines on the quote?

Frequently asked questions

Why does my First Alert VX3 go offline at night?

Usually the VX3 is mounted right where the Wi-Fi is weakest and loses the connection when the household network gets busy in the evening. A signal reading at the camera, a wired access point closer to it, or a PoE cable usually ends it.

Can First Alert cameras work without an alarm panel?

Yes. VX cameras and the doorbell can run in the Total Connect app with a video plan and no panel. If you do have a ProSeries touchscreen at the right firmware level, the cameras can also appear on it.

Do I need a town permit to add cameras to a Long Island house?

For cameras alone, usually not, but rules vary by town and village, so check yours. If the cameras link to a monitored alarm, the county alarm permit rules in Nassau or Suffolk still apply to that system.

Why do First Alert prices differ so much between dealers?

Because each authorized dealer sets its own pricing and often bundles equipment into a monitoring contract. Ask for equipment, labor and the monthly plan as separate numbers so you can compare quotes fairly.

Can a VX3 mount on brick or stucco with no wire inside the wall?

Yes. Resideo makes a surface back box for the VX3 that accepts conduit, so the cable runs along the outside of the wall in a protected pathway instead of inside it.
