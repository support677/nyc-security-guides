Condo Security Camera Installation Long Island boards usually come to the subject after something happened at night: a car opened in the guest lot, a mailbox kiosk pried, a gate arm snapped by a driver nobody saw. Island condos are spread across acres of parking courts, lanes and amenities, and most of the trouble happens after dark, so the camera plan should be judged by what it records at 2 a.m., not by a daytime demo. Below is a night-first way to plan, who pays when a master association is involved, and how to keep the system working through storms and snowbird season. Night is the real specification

Every brochure frame is shot at noon. On a Long Island condo property the useful question is what each spot looks like under sodium lamps, headlight glare or no light at all. Use this as a starting map for the property walk:

Infrared alone turns a red car and a black car into the same gray shape. Where the board can add a fixture, do it; where it cannot, color low-light cameras earn their extra cost on lots and lanes. Detection that tells people and vehicles apart cuts the false alerts that deer, wind and swaying hedges cause on open ground.

Plate cameras: worth it only at the right spot

License-plate capture is the most requested and most often disappointing item on Island condo wish lists. It works when the camera has a narrow view, a fast shutter and its own infrared, and is aimed where cars drop below roughly 15 miles per hour with the plate square to the lens — at a gate arm, a speed bump or the visitor lane. It fails on a wide entrance road where cars roll through at speed. A plate camera is a separate line on the quote; a board without a gate or a natural slow point may do better spending that money on lot coverage.

Who owns the ground the camera sits on

Many Long Island communities are layered: a master homeowners association owns the entrance road, clubhouse and pool, while one or more condominium associations own the buildings and their courts. Before anyone signs, settle three things in writing:

  • Which entity pays for which cameras. Ask for the gate, clubhouse and pool to be priced apart from each association's buildings so each board approves its own share.
  • Whose recorder holds which views. One recorder can serve both, with permissions split by camera group.
  • Limited common elements. A rear patio or deck may belong to one owner's use even though the association maintains it. Aim cameras so those areas, and every window, are out of frame or masked.

Owner-installed doorbells and floodlight cameras on shared porches raise the same question in reverse. Condo documents typically bar owners from altering common elements without consent, so the board's rules should say what owners may mount and where.

Storms, outages and the recorder

Long Island loses power in big storms — Tropical Storm Isaias in 2020 knocked out a large share of PSEG Long Island customers — and a recorder without backup stops exactly when looting or damage is likeliest. Ask for a battery backup on the recorder and every outdoor switch, put the recorder in a dry, ventilated room such as the clubhouse office rather than a low maintenance garage on the South Shore, and choose salt-tolerant housings within sight of the bay or ocean in Long Beach, Lido Beach or Freeport. Offline alerts sent to the agent's email whenever a camera or drive stops reporting mean nobody discovers a dead camera the morning after an incident.

Running the system when half the board winters south

Island boards tend to approve capital work over the summer and struggle to reach a quorum from December to March. Two habits help:

  1. Time the vote. Get the property walk and proposal done in spring so the board can vote before the pool season ends; a project that misses the summer often waits a year.
  2. Give remote access by role. The managing agent gets live view, playback and export through the app; a gatehouse login sees only entrance cameras with no export; board members get whatever level the board sets; maintenance sees equipment and storage areas only.

Police requests may come from Nassau County, a village department, Suffolk County or an East End town, depending on the community. Route every request to the managing agent and save a copy of the clip even when its release waits for counsel.

Frequently asked questions

What is the starting price for cameras at a Nassau or Suffolk condo?

The entry package is listed at $999 for Nassau communities and $1,099 for Suffolk ones: four house-brand cameras and a recorder, installed, with up to 100 feet of cable allowed per camera. Most communities need more than four cameras; each added camera, long run, trench or plate camera is priced after the property walk.

Can our cameras record license plates at the entrance?

Yes, if there is a place where cars slow below about 15 miles per hour with the plate facing the lens, such as a gate arm or speed bump. A dedicated plate camera with a narrow view and its own infrared is needed; a general overview camera will rarely read plates at night.

Do cameras actually stop car break-ins in condo lots?

Cameras record; lighting deters. Owners on homeowner forums say as much, and both are true. The best results come from pairing better lot lighting with color low-light cameras at each court entrance, so the footage shows a usable description when something does happen.

Can an owner put a camera on their own patio?

It depends on the declaration and the board's rules. Patios and decks are often limited common elements that the association maintains, and most condo documents require board consent before an owner alters common elements. A written owner-camera rule avoids disputes.

What if the gatehouse is staffed by a guard company?

Give the gatehouse its own login with live view of the entrance cameras and no export rights. Recordings stay under the managing agent's control, and the login can be reset whenever the guard contractor changes.
