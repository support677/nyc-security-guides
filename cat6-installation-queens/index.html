The access question each building raises

  • Houses. Is the basement ceiling open, and is there an attic? Open framing means horizontal runs out of sight and small openings in the walls. A finished basement with no access, or plaster on lath upstairs, adds hours.
  • Mixed-use over retail. Who controls the riser, and is the telecom room shared with other tenants? Your cable must be labeled and kept inside your own space. Noisy work often moves to evenings because the shops below are open late.
  • Towers. Insurance certificates, freight elevator time and sometimes a building cabling standard come before the first pull.
  • Industrial floors. Where will racking move next year? Tray has to clear forklift lanes after the next re-layout, and data cable should not run alongside big motor loads.
  • Near the airport. Some facilities need crew credentials arranged in advance. Ask on the first call, not the morning of.
  • Schools, agencies, hospitals. Certification reports are often written into the contract. Confirm the bidder will hand over test results for every link.

The August check: power on the cable in hot spaces

Cameras, access points and video intercoms now run on Power over Ethernet, which means the cable carries power as well as data. Heat raises a conductor's resistance, so less power arrives at the far end. Queens has two hot spots for this. One is the attic of a detached house, where a camera run tested in April can start rebooting in August. The other is a warehouse roof deck, where long runs sit in summer heat. Three things keep a run working year-round:

  • Solid copper conductors on every run. Copper-clad aluminum is cheaper by the box, cracks at terminations and loses more power.
  • Modest bundle sizes, so heat can escape.
  • A switch whose PoE budget covers every powered device running at the same time.

Cat6, Cat6A, or keep the Cat5e?

Cat6 is the right call for nearly everything that plugs into a wall: desks, handsets, cameras, radios. Cat6A is for 10-gigabit links between closets or to servers; it does not stretch the 100-meter channel limit, so it does not fix a long run. Existing Cat5e that tests clean can stay in service. If you replace it, new cable goes in, often along the old route.

What you should have when the crew leaves

  • A test result for every drop, taken before ceilings and walls closed.
  • Matching labels at both ends and a map from each panel port to its outlet.
  • Photos of any pathway that is now hidden.
  • Spare drops landed at the panel for the next hire or camera.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to run ethernet from the basement to a second-floor bedroom in a Queens house?

At the published Queens rate, one Cat6 drop up to 100 feet is $272, including the plate, jack, terminations and a test. Longer routes are measured and priced on the actual path.

Someone quoted me $500 per drop. Is that normal?

Per-drop quotes vary a lot depending on what they include. Compare line by line: cable length, terminations at both ends, testing, labeling, and whether racks or switches are folded in.

Do I need Cat6A for my home?

Usually not. Cat6 handles gigabit to every room and PoE cameras with margin. Cat6A matters for 10-gigabit links over long runs, which few homes use.

Can old Cat5e be upgraded by changing the jacks?

No. The rating belongs to the cable. Upgrading means pulling new cable, and the old route can often be reused.

Why does my attic camera drop offline in summer?

Heat raises the cable's resistance, so less PoE power reaches the camera. Copper-clad aluminum cable, long runs and tight bundles make it worse. A test at the panel shows whether the cable is the cause.

Can the work be done after hours in a building with stores downstairs?

Yes. Evening and weekend work is common in Queens mixed-use buildings, and the evening or weekend premium shows up as a separate line on the quote.
