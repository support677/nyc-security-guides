The sticker price on the panel and sensors is the small part; the bigger part is what you keep paying for monitoring, plus the county permit that most monitored systems need before police will respond. This guide walks through those costs in the order they hit you, then covers which system style suits a typical Nassau or Suffolk house. Step one: the county permit nobody mentions in the ad

Long Island is policed mostly at the county level, and both county police departments run alarm registration programs. Nassau County Police operate an Alarm Permit Section, and Suffolk County Police run an Alarm Management Program. If your system is monitored and the central station can request a police response, plan on registering the address. Some villages with their own police departments have separate rules, so check which department actually covers your street.

The reason these programs exist is false alarms. Both counties tie a permit to a limited number of false dispatches per permit year — Suffolk's own FAQ describes two false-alarm allowances per 365-day permit period — and fines can follow after that. Nassau will not let you renew online while false-alarm fines are outstanding. In practice, that means a sloppy install (loose door contacts, a motion detector staring at a forced-air vent) is not just annoying; it becomes a recurring bill. Fees change, so confirm the current amounts on the county police websites before you budget.

Step two: do the monitoring math over three years

National alarm brands commonly advertise a low or free equipment price in exchange for a multi-year monitoring contract. The honest way to compare offers is to add equipment, installation and every monthly payment over the full contract term, then compare totals.

The Abstract Enterprises Security Systems page lists its own Long Island numbers: a basic residential package starting at $600, a fuller home package starting at $2,500, a commercial package starting at $1,500, and central station monitoring at $30 a month for keypad-only or $45 a month with the phone app, with no long-term contract. Use those figures as one column in your comparison, and ask every other company for the same three things in writing: equipment total, monthly rate, and contract length with any early-cancellation terms.

Long Island housing is mostly single-family, but the build year changes everything about how sensors get wired. Here is how the common choices line up.

Whatever the style, insist on a cellular path to the monitoring center. Many Long Island homes dropped their copper phone lines years ago, and a panel that still dials out over a landline may be silently disconnected.

What happens on install day

  1. Walk-through. The installer confirms every exterior door, reachable window, basement hatch and garage entry against the written scope.
  2. Panel and power. The control panel goes somewhere protected, often a basement or utility closet, with a backup battery and the cellular communicator.
  3. Sensors. Door and window contacts, motion detectors and glass-break sensors are mounted; motion units are aimed away from heat sources, pets' paths and sunny windows.
  4. Keypads and app. Keypads go by the doors you actually use. App users are added and tested.
  5. Signal test with the central station. Every zone is tripped while the monitoring center confirms it received the right zone name.
  6. Training and permit paperwork. You learn arming modes and how to cancel a false alarm, then finish the county registration.

Questions to ask any Long Island alarm installer

  • What is your New York State Department of State license, and can I look it up? (Alarm installers in New York must be licensed.)
  • Is the panel proprietary, or can another company take over monitoring later if I switch?
  • How does the system reach the central station — cellular, internet, or both?
  • Who handles the Nassau or Suffolk permit, and what is your plan to keep false alarms down?
  • If I already own a panel, can you reuse my existing sensor wiring?
  • Is smoke and carbon monoxide detection included, and is it monitored?

A short scenario: the inherited 1990s panel

A common Long Island situation: you buy a 1960s split-level and find a keypad by the side door that has not worked since the previous owner cancelled service. Before replacing everything, have someone test the old door and window contacts. Wired contacts often outlive the panel, and a new panel with a cellular communicator can frequently be connected to them, which saves most of the labor of rewiring. If the panel was locked to a defunct company, a replacement panel is usually simpler than trying to unlock it.

Frequently asked questions

Does a monitored alarm on Long Island need a police permit?

If your system is monitored and police may be dispatched, usually yes. Nassau County Police run an Alarm Permit Section and Suffolk County Police run an Alarm Management Program. Villages with their own police may have separate rules, so check which department covers your address.

How many false alarms are allowed before fines start?

Suffolk County describes two false-alarm allowances per 365-day permit period for a registered account. Nassau also fines excessive false alarms and will not renew a permit online while fines are unpaid. Check the county police websites for current rules and fees.

What does a new alarm system cost on Long Island?

On its Long Island page, Abstract Enterprises Security Systems lists a basic residential package starting at $600, a fuller home package starting at $2,500 and a commercial package starting at $1,500, with monitoring at $30 a month keypad-only or $45 a month with the app and no long-term contract.

Wired or wireless — which is better for a Long Island house?

It depends on access. Homes with an unfinished basement are often easy to hardwire on the first floor, while finished two-story homes usually do best with a hybrid: wired downstairs, wireless upstairs. Rentals and condos usually go fully wireless.

Can someone install the Ring or SimpliSafe kit I already bought?

Yes. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems lists bring-your-own-equipment installation of Ring or SimpliSafe kits at $200 to $400 depending on the number of devices, including sensor placement, app setup and testing.

Can I keep my old alarm sensors when I switch companies?

Often. Hardwired door and window contacts frequently outlast the panel. A technician can test them and connect them to a new panel with a cellular communicator, which avoids most rewiring.
