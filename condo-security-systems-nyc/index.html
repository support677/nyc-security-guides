Condo security systems NYC boards look after sit on an unusual ownership map. Every owner holds a deed to a unit, while the lobby, the elevators, the garage and the amenity floors belong to all of them together. That split decides who approves a project, who pays for it and who gets blamed when the front door stops working. This guide follows the order that tends to save a board money: sort out ownership, price the work from published numbers, recover control from the developer, then write the rules for guests and footage. Start with the declaration, not the hardware

New York's Condominium Act leaves the fine print to each building's declaration and bylaws, so two condos on the same block can divide responsibility differently. Before anyone asks for bids, pull those documents and mark each device below. The middle column shows the common pattern, not a ruling; your own documents control.

DeviceUsually treated asWhere the argument starts
Lobby entrance panel, door strike, vestibule cameraCommon element, paid from common chargesRarely disputed
Riser cable climbing to each floorCommon elementOwners on a silent line assume the fix is theirs; it usually isn't
Intercom station or monitor inside the unitDepends on how the declaration draws the unit boundaryThe most frequent dispute between owners and the board
Fobs, cards and phone credentialsIssued by the board, often with a replacement feeLost-fob charges and how many credentials each unit receives
Elevator floor restrictionsCommon element, coordinated with the elevator contractorPenthouse and private-landing units
Burglar alarm inside a unitThe owner's own responsibilityOwners asking the association to monitor it

One habit prevents most of these fights: diagnose before assigning blame. When a whole floor or the entire building loses service, the cause is almost never one owner's equipment. A single quiet apartment might be. A paid diagnostic visit that names the failed part settles the question with evidence instead of a heated email thread.

What the board is really buying

Condo security is five systems that ought to share one list of users:

  • Entry. The street panel, apartment stations or app calling, and the door hardware behind them.
  • Credentials. Fobs, cards or phone keys. Many condos that opened ten or more years ago still run 125 kHz proximity fobs, which are cheap to copy. Moving to encrypted credentials means new readers and a new fob for every resident.
  • Elevators. Each credential limited to the floors its holder belongs on, with amenity floors opening on posted hours.
  • Cameras. Entrances, the package room, the garage ramp and the service door.
  • Association spaces. The management office, the super's workshop, the roof bulkhead and any commercial unit the condominium owns, which can carry a monitored alarm.

When all five answer to a single administrator list, a sale closes and one change removes the seller from the door, the elevator and the app at once.

The sponsor handover checklist

In a new condominium the developer usually buys and installs the original systems, then hands control to an owner-elected board. Security is easy to lose in that transition. Before the sponsor's people move on, collect:

  1. Administrator logins for the intercom platform, access software and video recorder, moved into the association's name.
  2. Every cloud account and license, with its renewal date.
  3. Warranty paperwork for each device and the date each one runs out.
  4. As-built drawings of doors, readers, cameras and cable paths.
  5. An equipment register listing model and serial numbers.
  6. The credential list, checked against the current owner roll, with orphaned fobs deleted.

Boards that skip this often discover, years later, that the only person able to add a fob works for a company that stopped answering. Once the equipment is identified, a service visit can usually recover or reset administrator access.

Renters, guests and stays under 30 days

Investor-owned units bring a steady flow of tenants and visitors. The technical tools are simple: a fob that stops working on the lease end date, a code good only at the front door during daytime, and a phone invitation the owner issues and cancels from an app. The policy belongs to the board. New York City limits most rentals shorter than 30 days and requires hosts to register under Local Law 18, and many declarations add leasing rules of their own. The access system should enforce what the board decided; it cannot stand in for that decision.

Scenario: a conversion condo in its second year

Picture a 60-unit prewar rental converted to condominium. The sponsor installed a new lobby panel but left the original riser cable in the walls. Within a year, one vertical line of apartments goes quiet. Those owners call the managing agent, the agent calls the board, and someone suggests each owner replace their own station. The diagnostic visit finds a break in the riser between two floors, a common element repair. The same visit turns up two more problems: the recorder login is still registered to the sponsor, and the fob list holds dozens of credentials nobody can match to an owner. The riser gets fixed, the accounts get moved and cleaned, and the board receives a written proposal to retire the cloneable fobs in the next budget year.

Frequently asked questions

Who pays to repair the intercom station inside a condo unit in NYC?

Your declaration and bylaws decide, through the way they define where a unit ends and the common elements begin. In some buildings the apartment station belongs to the owner; in others the entire intercom, stations included, counts as a building system. If a full line or floor is affected, the fault is usually in shared wiring, which is the association's.

Does a condo board need an owner vote to replace the building intercom?

In most buildings the board can repair or replace common elements within its budget authority, but some bylaws require an owner vote for improvements above a set amount. Check the bylaws and, for a large project, ask the building's counsel before signing.

How much does a building-wide condo security upgrade cost in NYC?

It is priced after a free on-site survey, because entrances, cable condition, monitors versus app and elevator scope all change the number. Published anchors include $1,200–$5,000+ for a 5 to 30 unit intercom and $3,000–$8,000 installed to move about 30 units to encrypted fobs.

Can a condo replace its intercom without work inside every unit?

Often, yes. If the existing riser cable tests sound, a new entrance panel can reuse it, and app-based calling can reduce or remove the need for new stations in each apartment. A survey confirms which path the building can take.

How quickly can a technician come out?

A call, text or web form gets the appointment set within two hours on any day of the week, and the technician arrives inside a two-hour window. Same-day arrival is not promised; early slots go in the order service calls are paid.
