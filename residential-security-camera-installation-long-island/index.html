Residential Security Camera Installation Long Island: Plan the Whole Lot Before You Count Cameras

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-13

Residential security camera installation Long Island projects go wrong in a predictable way: the homeowner counts cameras before anyone has walked the yard. On a quarter-acre lot in Nassau or a two-acre parcel in Suffolk, the house is only part of what you are protecting. The driveway, the side gate, the pool fence and the detached garage often matter more than the front door. This guide gives you a lot-first way to plan, so you can judge any quote with confidence. Start with a sketch, not a shopping cart

Print an aerial view of your property from any free map tool and mark five things in pen: every door a person can open from outside, the path a car takes from the street to where it parks, any outbuilding, any gate in the fence, and the spots where the yard goes dark at night. Those marks are your camera plan. Everything else — brand, resolution, the app — is a detail you settle afterward.

Most Long Island homes end up with four to eight marks. A cape on a tight Levittown lot may need only three. A Huntington colonial with a pool and a separate garage, or an East End house on a deep lot, can need a dozen once the back fence line is included.

Notice the pattern: the zones closest to the house go on cable, and only the far edges rely on batteries or solar. That hybrid layout is what most Long Island properties settle on, because Wi-Fi from a router in the den rarely reaches the back corner of a big lot with any consistency.

What weather does to cameras out here

Long Island hardware lives through nor'easters, freeze-thaw cycles and, near the water, salt in the air for much of the year. That has three practical consequences. First, ask for weather ratings of IP66 or IP67 on anything outdoors. Second, mount cameras under a soffit or eave wherever the view allows, so rain does not streak the lens. Third, make sure cable entries are sealed with drip loops, because water follows a cable into a wall faster than most people expect. On waterfront homes, check any metal brackets and junction boxes every spring for corrosion.

Seasonal and second homes

A summer place out east or a house you leave empty for the winter changes the job. The cameras matter less than the remote side of the system. Before you sign off on an install for a vacant home, confirm these points:

  • The recorder and router sit on a small battery backup so a brief outage does not take the whole system offline.
  • Alerts are tuned to people and vehicles, so deer, raccoons and blowing branches do not train you to ignore your phone.
  • Someone local has the app and can check on the house if you are traveling.
  • The recorder keeps enough days of footage to cover the gap between your visits.

Cost: what moves the number

Two homes with the same camera count can get very different quotes. The biggest drivers on Long Island are the length of the cable runs (especially out to a garage or pool house), how the cable gets into the house (an unfinished basement or open attic is quicker than finished ceilings), how many cameras need high resolution for faces and plates, and whether you want a local recorder or a cloud plan. Six things to ask before you sign

  1. Is the price based on a visit that covered the whole lot, outbuildings included?
  2. Which zones will be wired, and which will be wireless or solar — and why?
  3. Where will the recorder live, and how many days will it hold?
  4. How will cable enter the house, and will any wire be left visible on the siding?
  5. Is audio recording on or off, and how will you keep cameras from looking into a neighbor's windows?
  6. Do you hold the New York State license required for security system work?

That fifth question matters more than people think. Under New York's eavesdropping law, capturing other people's private conversations without consent is illegal, which is why outdoor microphones are normally switched off. Aim cameras at your own property, and if you live in a community with an HOA or condo board, check its rules on exterior attachments before mounting anything on shared walls.

A typical install day

On a mid-sized home the work usually fits in a single day. The crew confirms the camera spots with you, runs cable through the attic, basement or crawl space, mounts and seals each camera, then sets up the recorder and connects it to your network. The last hour should be spent with you at the app: checking each view, adjusting motion zones so the street is ignored, and making sure you can pull up a recorded clip yourself.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to have security cameras installed on a Long Island house?

It depends mostly on the property: cable length to outbuildings, how cable gets into the house and how many cameras need high resolution. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems lists its package pricing and home-size tiers on its Long Island residential page and gives a fixed price after a walk-through.

Can an installer put in cameras I already bought?

Yes. Many installers, Abstract Enterprises Security Systems included, will install customer-supplied cameras. Ask first whether your cameras support wired PoE or rely on Wi-Fi, because that decides how reliable the far corners of the lot will be.

Should cameras on a big lot be wired or wireless?

Wire the zones near the house — doors, driveway, side passage and pool — because cable carries power and video and does not drop. Use solar or battery cameras only at the far edges, such as a dock or back fence.

Do home camera systems need a monthly fee?

Not if footage is stored on a local recorder in the house. Cloud plans charge per camera or per month; a local recorder keeps footage at home with free phone viewing.

How many days of footage will a home recorder keep?

Most home recorders are set up for roughly two to four weeks, and a larger drive extends that. Use a storage calculator with your camera count and resolution to check.

Can my cameras record audio?

New York's eavesdropping law bars capturing private conversations without a participant's consent, so outdoor audio is normally switched off. Keep cameras aimed at your own property and away from a neighbor's windows.
