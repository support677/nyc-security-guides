Write the ticket before anyone drives up the Thruway

A short, specific description lets the technician bring the right tester, cable and parts on the first trip. Send these details by email or text when you book:

  • Where: the site, the building, the floor and the closet that feeds the problem area.
  • What: outlet numbers from the wall plates, patch panel ports if you can read them, and the devices affected (a phone, a camera, an access point, a register).
  • When: the date it started and anything that happened that week, such as a storm, a contractor in the ceiling, a move or a new piece of equipment.
  • Pattern: one outlet, one room, one wing or a whole building; constant or on and off.
  • Access: who has keys to the closets, whether ceilings are reachable, and any hours when work cannot happen.

Photos of the closet and of any labels help more than you would expect. Calls in the three southern counties, Westchester, Rockland and Putnam, are usually reached on the day or by the following morning. Ulster, Dutchess and Orange are booked into set windows, which is where a complete ticket pays off most.

When the building is new and the network still fails

A failure in a building that opened last year often points back to the original installation, not wear. Drops get terminated in a hurry before a move-in date, test results are never saved, or a cheaper continuity checker was used in place of a certification tester. A continuity checker confirms that the wires are connected in the right order; it does not measure length, loss or crosstalk, which are what cause slow links and dropped connections.

If a new building has repeated problems, ask the original installer for the saved certification results for every drop. If they cannot produce them, a certification audit, testing every run and documenting what passes and what fails, gives the owner a clear list to take back to the installer and their warranty, rather than paying for repairs one outlet at a time.

When a repair should become a redesign

Most faults are local: a bad termination, a crushed run, a failed jack or a damaged patch cord. Those get fixed, tested and documented on the same visit. A few findings mean the design itself is the problem, and fixing the same spot again will not help:

  • The run is longer than the 100-meter channel limit. Pulling a fresh cable along that route just repeats the failure; the outlet or the switch has to move.
  • Copper links two separate buildings and keeps failing after storms. The lasting fix is usually fiber, which carries no electrical current between structures.
  • A cable passes only by a small margin. It may work today and drop out on the next hot day in the ceiling; replacement is cheaper than repeat visits.
  • A powered device keeps rebooting on a long run. The PoE budget or cable gauge may need rethinking, not just a new jack.

Choosing who to call

Lead marketplaces pass one request to several contractors, and the person who calls back first is not always the one with a certification tester in the van. Ask any company three things: whether their own technician will do the work, what tester they use and whether you get saved results, and how quickly they reach your county. A sample test report is a good sign that documentation is routine for them.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the Ethernet run to my garage or barn keep failing after storms?

Copper between separate buildings can pick up surges when the ground potential at each building differs during a storm. Surge protection helps, but the lasting fix on a link that keeps failing is usually fiber, which carries no current between buildings.

How fast can a network cable repair be scheduled in the Hudson Valley?

In Westchester, Putnam and Rockland most calls are reached on the day or by the next morning. Visits in Ulster, Dutchess and Orange go into set windows, so put every problem outlet on the ticket.

My cable passed a cheap tester. Why is it still slow?

A basic continuity tester only checks that the wires connect in the right order. It cannot measure length, loss or crosstalk, which are what cause slow or dropping links. A certification tester shows those.

Can a damaged network cable be spliced?

A cut run inside a wall can be rejoined with a rated coupler or brought to a new jack, though each joint costs signal. The whole channel should be tested afterwards, and if the margin is thin the run should be replaced.
