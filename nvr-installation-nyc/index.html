NVR Installation NYC: Work Backward From the Footage You Need, Then Pick the Box

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-11

NVR Installation NYC jobs go wrong in a predictable way: someone buys the recorder first and asks how much footage it holds later. This guide flips that order. You start with the number of days you must be able to go back, work out channels and storage from there, decide where the box can physically live in your building, and only then choose hardware. If you would rather hand the whole thing off, our NVR Installation NYC page covers what Abstract Enterprises Security Systems installs and configures across the five boroughs.

Step 1: Decide the retention number first

Retention is the single figure that drives everything else. Ask who is going to come asking for video and how late they tend to ask. Package-theft complaints usually arrive within days; chargebacks and insurance or injury letters can come weeks or a month later. Thirty days is a common floor for small businesses.

Next, count cameras now and cameras you will probably add. Almost nobody stays at the first count, so a recorder with no spare inputs becomes a replacement project the first time you want one more view of the back door. AESS plans 25 to 50 percent spare channels for that reason.

Step 2: Turn days into terabytes

Storage depends on resolution, frame rate, compression (H.265 is far lighter than H.264), and whether cameras record around the clock or only on motion. As a rough planning figure, the AESS NVR page puts one continuously recording 4K camera at about 40 to 60 GB per day, which means eight 4K cameras held for 30 days lands somewhere around 8 to 12 TB. To run your own numbers, plug your cameras into the free security camera storage calculator and compare the result with the drive bays on any recorder you are considering.

Then ask what happens when a drive dies. Surveillance drives write nonstop and eventually wear out. A two-drive mirror (RAID 1) or a parity array (RAID 5 or 6) on a larger unit lets one drive fail without losing anything. For a home with a few cameras a single drive may be an acceptable risk; for a store, a multi-family building or a warehouse it usually is not.

Step 4: Find the recorder a home in your building

Common spots and their trade-offs:

  • Basement or cellar of a brownstone or row house. Short runs to the rear yard and cellar door, but keep the unit off the floor where there is any history of water, and away from the boiler.
  • Super's closet or meter room in a walk-up or elevator building. Usually where the risers start, so cabling is easier. Get written permission from the owner or managing agent, and make sure the closet locks.
  • Back office of a storefront. Convenient for the owner, but a recorder sitting next to the register is the first thing a burglar takes. A locked cabinet or a less obvious shelf helps.
  • IT room in an office building. Rack space and network are already there; expect to coordinate with the building's IT staff.

Wherever it goes, the box needs airflow, a dedicated outlet, and ideally a small battery backup so a brief outage or brownout does not interrupt recording or corrupt a drive.

Step 5: Plan remote viewing around your actual internet

Reddit is full of recorders that will not stay connected to the phone app. In the city the culprit is often the internet plan: some providers and many 5G home plans share one public address among customers, so classic port forwarding cannot work, and other lines change address without warning. The fix is usually the manufacturer's P2P service, a VPN to the building, or a business line with a static address. Whatever route is chosen, every default password gets changed and unused services get switched off, because recorders left open to the internet are scanned and broken into routinely.

What install day looks like

  1. Walk the building with the owner, super or office manager.
  2. Mount the recorder and switch, connect power and battery backup, and land each camera cable on a labeled port.
  3. Install and format drives, set up RAID if specified, and confirm the reported capacity.
  4. Add each camera, name it by location, set resolution, frame rate and recording schedule, and draw motion zones.
  5. Set the clock and time zone and sync it to a time server, so exported clips carry trustworthy timestamps.
  6. Configure remote access, install the app on the owner's phones, and test from outside the building's network.
  7. Export a short test clip together, so you know how to hand video to police or an insurer before you ever need to.

Handover checklist

  • Admin password stored somewhere other than a sticky note on the recorder
  • Separate user logins for staff, the super or a management company, with view-only rights where appropriate
  • A one-page map showing which port feeds which camera
  • The calculated days of retention, and a reminder to check the oldest recording date every month

Frequently asked questions

How much hard drive space does an NVR need?

It depends on camera count, resolution, compression and how many days you keep. As a planning figure, one 4K camera recording continuously uses roughly 40 to 60 GB a day, so eight 4K cameras for 30 days is around 8 to 12 TB. Motion-only recording and H.265 reduce that. Run your own numbers in a storage calculator before buying drives.

Can I use cameras from a different brand on my NVR?

Often yes, through the ONVIF standard, but not always with every feature. Basic video usually works; smart detection and some alerts may only work when camera and recorder are the same brand. Confirm compatibility before buying.

Why can't I see my NVR cameras from my phone when I'm away?

In NYC the usual causes are an internet plan that uses carrier-grade NAT, a public IP that keeps changing, or a router that lost its settings. Switching to the maker's P2P service, a VPN, or a static-IP business line typically solves it.

Do I need RAID on a home camera system?

Not always. For a few home cameras a single drive is a fair risk; for a business or multi-family building, a mirrored or parity array is worth it.

Where should the NVR go in a NYC apartment building?

Usually in a locked space near where the cable risers start, such as a super's closet, meter room or basement utility area, with the owner's written permission, a dedicated outlet, airflow and a battery backup.

Are there monthly fees with an NVR?

No subscription is required for local recording. The recorder stores video on its own drives, and remote viewing through the manufacturer's app is normally free.
