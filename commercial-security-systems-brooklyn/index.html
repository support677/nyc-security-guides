First, take stock of what came with the space

Inherited equipment can save real money or cost you weeks, depending on whether anyone can still log into it. Before you plan anything new, go down this list with the landlord or the previous tenant.

Inherited itemWhat to getWhat goes wrong if you don't
Camera recorderThe admin username and password, or permission to factory-reset itYou can see live video on the monitor but can't export a clip for police
Alarm panelThe installer code and the name of the monitoring companyThe old account keeps calling the prior tenant, or nobody at all
Card or fob readersThe software login and a list of active credentialsFormer employees of the last business can still open your door
Intercom or buzzerWho services it and whether it is tied to the building's systemYou replace something the landlord was responsible for
Cable in the ceilingWhere the runs go and whether they test cleanNew cable gets pulled next to perfectly good cable

Equipment you can't log into is not useless. Most recorders and many panels can be reset and adopted by a licensed installer, which is often cheaper than starting over.

Questions to put in writing before you sign

  1. How many days of video will the recorder hold at the camera count and resolution quoted?
  2. For each door and gate: what happens in a power outage, does it lock or unlock?
  3. Who owns the hardware, and who holds the administrator passwords once the job is done?
  4. Which monthly charges are optional, and can monitoring be month to month?
  5. What does a service call cost after the warranty ends?
  6. Will the installer send a certificate of insurance to the landlord or managing agent before work starts? Most Brooklyn commercial landlords ask for one.
  7. What is excluded? A short exclusions list is a warning sign, not a good sign.

The monthly side of the bill

With Abstract Enterprises Security Systems you own the equipment and there are no required monthly fees. Costs only appear where you choose them. Central-station alarm monitoring is listed at about $40 to $120 a month, depending on zones, cellular backup and open and close reporting. If you want operators watching yard or lot cameras live, that is priced on its own, at $800 to $2,500 monthly. Once the job is handed over, a service call is billed at the published $195 hourly rate, three hours minimum, so $585 for a short visit. Each system is covered for 30 days, and you can buy a longer plan of one to five years if you want one.

Alarm habits that keep you out of trouble

A badly tuned alarm costs more than its monitoring fee. Motion sensors aimed at a heater, a door contact that rattles when the roll-down gate shakes, or a staff member who never learned the disarm code all lead to false alarms, and repeated ones strain your relationship with the monitoring center and the responding precinct. For fire systems, New York City can bill a building for repeated unwarranted alarms. Three habits help: give each employee their own code so you can see who armed and disarmed, ask for open and close reports so you know the store was locked at night, and have the installer walk the zones with you before the system goes live.

A Brooklyn takeover, step by step

A coffee shop takes over a former phone store on a Flatbush corridor. The space has six cameras, a recorder in the back closet and an alarm still reporting to the previous tenant's account. On the walkthrough, five cameras work and one points at a wall that no longer has a counter. The recorder is reset and adopted, the stray camera is moved to cover the register, and two new cameras go in the basement where deliveries are stored. The old alarm panel stays but gets a cellular communicator and a fresh monitoring account in the new owner's name, plus a glass-break sensor behind the gate. The back door gets a keypad so the morning baker doesn't need a key. The owner ends up with a written list of every password, held by her, not the installer.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a Brooklyn business budget for a security system?

Most single-location businesses in the published Brooklyn brackets fall between $5,000 and $22,000 installed, which covers cameras, an alarm with monitoring and one or two badge-controlled doors. A basic storefront can start around $3,000, while warehouses and multi-building sites begin near $18,000 and climb past $65,000.

Can I use the cameras and alarm the last tenant left behind?

Often. A licensed installer can usually reset and adopt an orphaned recorder or alarm panel. Get the passwords and the monitoring company's name if you can, and have the cable tested before anyone pulls new runs.

Do I have to pay for monitoring?

No. Monitoring is optional. Central-station alarm monitoring is listed at about $40 to $120 a month, and having operators watch yard cameras live is priced separately. Cameras recording to a local recorder have no monthly fee.

How much does an access-controlled door cost?

A typical commercial door, with the reader, electric lock hardware, door sensor, exit sensor and setup, is listed at roughly $1,500 to $3,000 installed. A simple door with cable already in place can cost less; glass storefront doors and fire-rated doors cost more.

What happens if something stops working after installation?

Problems in the first 30 days are covered by the warranty, and longer paid plans of one to five years are available. Without a plan, a visit is billed at the published hourly rate of $195, three hours minimum.

Will my landlord need paperwork before the work starts?

Most Brooklyn commercial landlords and managing agents ask for a certificate of insurance before an installer starts. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems issues COIs before mobilizing.
