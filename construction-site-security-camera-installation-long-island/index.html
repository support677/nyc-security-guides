Construction Site Security Camera Installation Long Island jobs look alike from the road, but the right setup comes down to three plain questions: what power the lot has right now, whether a cellular signal can carry video out of it, and where people and trucks get in. Answer those and the choice between rental units, a wired system or a trailer mostly makes itself. Pricing and booking are on our Construction Site Security Camera Installation Long Island page.

Question one: what power does the site have, and when?

A freshly cleared lot in Suffolk has nothing to plug into. A commercial pad in Nassau may get a temporary service pole within weeks. A gut renovation of an existing house still has the house's own panel. Each points to a different kind of camera.

  • No power yet: solar-and-battery units with their own cellular connection. They go up on a post or a temporary mast in one visit and need no electrician.
  • Temporary power on site: wired cameras powered over network cable from a small switch and recorder in the job trailer or a locked box. More reliable at night and in long cloudy stretches.
  • Existing building being renovated: wired cameras inside and at the doors, often on the same cable paths the permanent system will use later.

Solar units on Long Island have to be sized for short December days, not July. Ask how many cloudy days the battery is rated to cover and how the unit reports a low charge.

Question two: will a cell signal carry video off this lot?

Strong phone service at the curb does not guarantee a camera can upload video steadily from the back of the parcel. Wooded lots, low spots, the edges of the Pine Barrens and parts of the North and South Forks are where this shows up. A careful installer tests upload speed from the actual mounting spot, on more than one carrier, before the unit is committed. If the signal is weak, the fixes are a taller mast, an external antenna, a different carrier SIM, or recording locally on the unit and sending only alerts and short clips.

Question three: where does everything come in and go out?

Thieves leave the same way trucks do. On most Long Island jobs that is one or two gates, plus any spot where the fence is easy to step over. The entrance camera does the heaviest lifting: it should be close enough and low enough to read a plate and show a face, not just a vehicle shape. Then cover what is valuable and stationary: the container, staged materials, equipment parked overnight and, on residential jobs, the house once windows and appliances go in. Wide shots of empty dirt add little.

How the layout changes by job type

Subdivision or multi-lot tract

Several houses at different stages, one or two road entrances, and materials dropped at each lot. Cover the entrances first, then move units from lot to lot as framing finishes and finished houses become the target.

Single custom or spec home

Usually one driveway, a dumpster and a garage that becomes storage once it is closed in. Two or three positions often do it, with one watching the driveway and front door once the house can be locked.

Commercial pad or retail build-out

Larger perimeter, deliveries at odd hours and a general contractor who wants progress views as well as security. Wired cameras on temporary power usually make sense, and the cable can be planned so it becomes part of the finished building's system.

Settle these with the owner and GC before install day

  • Who receives alerts after hours, and who is allowed to call the police or speak through a talk-down speaker?
  • How long is footage kept, and who can export a clip for police or the insurer?
  • Which cameras face neighboring yards or houses? Aim them at the site, and use privacy masks where a view crosses a property line.
  • Who moves cameras when the site layout changes, and does that cost extra?
  • What happens at closeout: removal, or conversion to a permanent system?

From jobsite cameras to the finished building

On commercial projects, the jobsite system does not have to disappear at turnover. If the permanent camera locations are known early, wired jobsite cameras can sit on cable that is already where the finished system needs it, and the building has coverage from certificate of occupancy to opening day. Rental units come off at closeout; removal is included on our rentals.

Frequently asked questions

Can cameras work on a lot with no electricity?

Yes. Solar-and-battery units with their own cellular connection are made for that. On Long Island they need to be sized for short winter days, and the cell signal should be tested from the actual mounting spot before the unit is committed.

Are cheap Wi-Fi cameras good enough for a jobsite?

Usually not. Most sites have no Wi-Fi, consumer cameras rely on it, and they are easy to pull down. Theft also happens at night, when alerts that nobody watches do little. Jobsite units with cellular service, local recording and monitoring are built for this.

Is monitored video cheaper than a night guard?

Often. Our Long Island page puts a single overnight guard at $3,000 to $6,000 or more a month, while monitored rental units start at $349 per month per unit. A guard can physically intervene, which cameras cannot, so some sites use both.

Will footage help with police reports and insurance claims?

It helps most when the entrance camera can show a plate or a face and when someone can export a clip quickly with the correct date and time. Agree before the job starts on who exports footage and how long it is kept.

Where do the cameras go once the job is done?

Rental units are removed at closeout. Wired cameras can be taken out or kept as part of the building's permanent system if the cable was planned for it.
