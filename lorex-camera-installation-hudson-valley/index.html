Lorex Camera Installation Hudson Valley calls come from a lot of people who are not at the house when something happens. They live in the city or out of state, drive up on Friday, and want the cameras to tell them on Tuesday that the place is fine. That changes the job. A system that only has to look good on install day is easy; one that still records after a three-day outage, with nobody there to reboot it, takes planning. This guide is written for part-time owners and anyone with a barn, a gate or a long drive. Current county prices sit on the Lorex Camera Installation Hudson Valley page, broken out county by county.

The question that decides everything: who notices when it stops?

At a full-time house, a dead camera gets noticed at dinner. At a weekend place it can be dark for a week before anyone opens the app. So the first design goal is not resolution or night colour. It is that the system reports its own failure. Ask for three things: a phone alert when a camera goes offline, a second person on the account who also gets that alert, and a habit of opening playback, not just live view, once a week. Playback is where a full or failing hard drive shows up.

Put the recorder where the house stays warm and dry

A Lorex wired system runs off its NVR. Every camera draws power from it or from a switch beside it, and every clip lives on its drive. That makes its location the most important decision on the job. The best spot is usually a heated interior closet or a finished basement corner, close to the router, away from sump pits and away from the attic, where summer heat cooks hard drives. An unheated barn or a seasonal porch is the wrong place even when it is the shortest cable run.

Test the outage, don't assume it

The valley is served by Con Edison, NYSEG, Central Hudson and Orange & Rockland, depending on the town, and long storm outages are part of owning a house up here. The Abstract Enterprises Security Systems page describes checking every install by cutting power at the main breaker and watching the recorder, router and switch carry on. That test is worth asking for by name, wherever you buy. It answers three questions in five minutes:

  • Does the battery backup actually hold the recorder, the PoE switch and the router together?
  • When power returns, does everything come back in the right order without a person pressing buttons?
  • If you have a standby generator, is the network closet on one of its circuits? Many transfer switches only carry a short list.

Reaching the barn, the gate and the far end of the drive

Lorex lists 328 feet as the longest Ethernet run between a recorder and a camera. On a five-acre parcel the farthest camera is often past that. The fix depends on what is at the far end:

Rodents, salt and the cable nobody looks at

One of the most common Lorex threads online is a camera that died because mice chewed the cable in a garage or crawlspace. Barns, woodsheds and stone foundations make that more likely here. Ask for cable to run in conduit or loom wherever it crosses an unfinished space, and for the jacket to be rated for the place it lives: outdoor or burial grade outside, plenum or riser only where the code calls for it. Road salt is another one: a camera mounted low by a plowed drive gets sprayed all winter, so mount it higher and angle it down.

Thin internet: what remote viewing needs

Recording is local and works with no internet at all. Watching from the city is a different matter. Plenty of houses in the upper valley still run on DSL, a cellular router or a satellite service. Those connections are fine for alerts and short clips but struggle with several 4K streams. Set the app to use the sub-stream for remote live view and keep the main stream for local recording. If you are on a cellular plan, check its data cap before turning on continuous cloud uploads.

A note on the company

Dahua, the Chinese camera maker, held Lorex until late 2022, when Skywatch bought it. Several state attorneys general have since sued Lorex over its security claims. Those suits are allegations, not findings. Lorex itself tells NDAA-bound buyers not to purchase its products, so a library, school or fire company should choose another brand; for a private house it is your call.

Frequently asked questions

What does a four-camera Lorex system cost in the valley?

Installed four-camera wired systems start at $2,033 for Westchester and top out at $2,299 for Dutchess and Ulster, with Rockland, Orange and Putnam in between, per Abstract Enterprises Security Systems published pricing.

How far can a Lorex camera be from the recorder?

Lorex gives 328 feet as the maximum Ethernet run. Past that you add a PoE switch, a fiber leg or a wireless bridge, or use a battery camera.

Do wired Lorex cameras keep going when the power fails?

They do as long as the recorder, its PoE switch and the router stay powered. A battery backup on all three, or a generator circuit that includes the network closet, keeps wired cameras running.

Why are some of my Lorex cameras offline after the power came back?

Often the recorder booted before the internet returned. Restart the modem and router first, wait a few minutes, then restart the NVR. If cameras are black on the local monitor too, the problem is power or cabling.

Can you install a Lorex kit I already bought?

Yes. You pay labour and any extra cable the property needs. Kit cables are often too short for valley lots.

Can I watch my Hudson Valley cameras from the city on slow internet?

Usually yes. Set remote viewing to the lower-resolution sub-stream and keep full resolution for local recording.
