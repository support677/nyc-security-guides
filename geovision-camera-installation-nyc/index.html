GeoVision Camera Installation NYC: Triage the System You Inherited Before You Buy a Single Camera

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-10

GeoVision Camera Installation NYC rarely begins with an empty wall. Far more often it starts with a box in a cellar closet or the super's apartment that says GeoVision on the front, a tangle of coax or network cable that nobody can name, and an owner, managing agent or co-op board who just wants to know three things: does it still work, is it safe to leave plugged in, and what should replace it when the time comes. This guide is a triage plan for that situation. It puts the cheap, decisive checks first and the spending last.

Step 1: read every label before touching anything

Photograph the front, back and label of each recorder, video server and camera you can reach. Write down model numbers. You are looking for two things: whether the system is a PC-based recorder (an ordinary-looking computer running GeoVision software, sometimes with capture cards for older analog cameras) or a standalone NVR or video server, and whether any device is on the end-of-life list from late 2024.

That list matters. Researchers tracking a botnet reported attacks on a pre-authentication flaw, CVE-2024-11120, in unsupported GeoVision devices; the models listed in published write-ups were the GV-VS12, GV-VS11, GV-DSP LPR V3 and GV-LX4C V2 and V3. In 2025 the federal cybersecurity agency added GeoVision flaws to its catalog of known exploited vulnerabilities. If your label matches, pull the device's network cable today. Leave its power on, so the disk is not disturbed, and treat replacement as scheduled work rather than an emergency.

Step 2: find out what the internet can see

With the owner's permission, log in to the building's router and look for port-forwarding rules that point at the recorder. Many older systems were set up so the previous super could watch from home by opening a port — convenient then, an open door now. Also note whether the recorder still uses the installer's original login. Closing those ports and changing credentials costs almost nothing and removes the biggest risk while you decide everything else.

Step 4: protect the footage before any reset

Lost passwords are the most common reason people search for GeoVision help, and the advice online is a mix of pin-hole reset tricks for other brands and step-by-step menus for GeoVision's PC software. The safer order is the same either way: decide whether the drive holds video that a tenant, an insurer or the police may ask for, export or preserve it, and only then reset. A factory reset can wipe settings and make earlier recordings difficult to reach. If the unit is end-of-life anyway, recovering access may not be worth the hours.

Step 5: plan the new cameras around the building, not the brand

New York's housing is old — more than 80 percent of it is over fifty years old, according to the Citizens Housing & Planning Council — and that shapes the cable route more than any camera spec does. In a prewar walk-up, each run goes up the stairwell inside surface raceway and has to be drilled through brick to reach the cellar. In an elevator building they ride risers. In a mixed-use building, the store and the residential side may have different owners and different hours. A good plan names each camera's job — face at the entry door, mailboxes, roof door, rear yard — and then the route each cable takes to get there.

What moves the price in each borough

Abstract Enterprises Security Systems prices GeoVision work by borough instead of quoting one citywide figure. Brooklyn and the Bronx carry the lowest labor rate. Queens adds roughly 5 percent, Staten Island roughly 10, and Manhattan roughly 25, mostly because of travel time, building access and parking. Hardware costs do not change from borough to borough. Within a borough, the swing factors are camera count, how far and through what each cable travels, fire-stopping at floor penetrations, recorder and drive size, building work hours, and paperwork such as a certificate of insurance for a board.

Questions to ask any installer about an inherited GeoVision system

  • Which of our existing devices are you keeping, and why?
  • Did you check every model against the end-of-life list and the router for open ports?
  • How will we view cameras remotely without port-forwarding?
  • Where exactly does each new cable run, and which penetrations get fire-stopped?
  • How many days will the recorder keep at our resolution?
  • Who gets their own login, and what do you hand over at the end — passwords, a port map, a camera list?

Frequently asked questions

Nobody has the password to our old GeoVision recorder. Can it be reset?

Often, but how depends on the system. Older GeoVision setups ran on a Windows PC with GeoVision software and capture cards, and resetting those is done through the software rather than a pin-hole button. Before any reset, decide whether footage on the drive needs to be saved, because a reset can make older recordings hard to reach.

Which GeoVision devices were hit by the 2024 botnet?

Reports on CVE-2024-11120 named end-of-life models including the GV-VS12, GV-VS11, GV-DSP LPR V3 and GV-LX4C V2 and V3. If your label matches, unplug the device's network cable, leave it powered so the drive is not disturbed, and plan to replace it.

Can GeoVision software record cameras from other brands?

GeoVision describes its GV-VMS software as recording video from both GeoVision and third-party IP devices, so a mixed building may not need a full rip-out. Whether a specific older camera works depends on its model and firmware, which is checked on site.

Is it safe to open a port on the router so we can view the cameras from our phones?

Opening ports to an old recorder is exactly how internet-facing devices get found and attacked. Remote viewing should be rebuilt through a supported app or secure connection, with the port-forwards removed and default passwords changed.

Should we keep any of the old GeoVision cameras?

Supported cameras with current firmware, a usable picture and sound cable can often stay. End-of-life units, cameras that cannot be updated and runs nobody can identify are the usual candidates for replacement.
