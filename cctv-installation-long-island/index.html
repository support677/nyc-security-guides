CCTV Installation Long Island projects go wrong in a predictable way: someone counts doors, buys a four-pack, and only later notices that the driveway, the shed and the dock were never in frame. A closed-circuit system on a Nassau or Suffolk property is really three decisions made in order: what the cameras need to see across the whole lot, which cable gets each camera its signal and power, and where the recorder sits so footage survives a storm. This guide walks through those decisions, then covers seasonal homes, install day and the published pricing for CCTV Installation Long Island from Abstract Enterprises Security Systems.

Start with a lot walk, not a camera count

Before anyone talks about megapixels, walk the property the way a stranger would approach it. On a typical Island lot that means the street edge, the driveway mouth, the side gate, the back yard, any detached garage or shed, and, on the South Shore canals or North Shore harbors, the bulkhead and dock. For each spot, write down one job for the camera: identify a face at the door, read a plate at the curb, or simply show that someone crossed the yard.

Those jobs decide lens and placement far more than brand does. A camera meant to recognize faces belongs at roughly head height plus a few feet, close to the door, not high on a second-floor soffit. A plate camera needs a narrow view where cars slow down. A yard camera can be wide. Once every spot has a job, the camera count falls out on its own, and it is usually more than four on a detached house with a garage.

Coax, Cat6 or Wi-Fi: matching the cable to the property

Older Long Island houses and strip-center stores often still have coax from an analog system installed fifteen or twenty years ago. New work is usually Cat6. Wi-Fi cameras still have a place, just not as the backbone.

The practical rule: if a cable can reach it, wire it. If the coax in the walls tests clean, reuse it with HD-analog cameras and save the labor of pulling new wire. A short wireless bridge can carry a signal out to a boathouse when trenching is not an option.

Where the recorder and battery belong

The recorder is the evidence locker, so treat it that way. A basement utility area or a closet away from the front door works; the top of the TV stand in plain view does not, because a burglar can carry it out with the footage inside. Plug the recorder, PoE switch and internet modem into a single battery backup (UPS). Overhead lines across the Island come down in nor'easters and summer storms, and a system that goes dark with the first flicker of the lights records nothing when it matters. A UPS will not run for days, but it rides through short outages and lets the recorder shut down cleanly in long ones instead of corrupting the drive.

Size the drive for at least two weeks of recording. Burglaries at an empty house are often discovered days later, and an insurance adjuster or detective will ask for footage from a specific night. For a rough number, plug your camera count, resolution and recording mode into the free security camera storage calculator to see the drive size.

Seasonal homes and boats: recording when nobody is there

East End and Fire Island-area houses that sit empty from fall to spring need a different setup than a year-round home. The system has to keep working without anyone resetting it, so a few extras earn their cost:

  • A scheduled weekly reboot on the recorder, so a frozen network stack recovers on its own.
  • Health alerts sent to your phone when a camera drops or the hard drive reports an error, not just motion alerts.
  • One indoor camera covering the main hallway or the basement stairs, which shows a forced door or water on the floor.
  • Stainless or coated mounting hardware on anything facing the water, and drip loops at every cable entry.

For a boat at a private dock, aim one camera along the finger pier with the light behind it, not into it. Glare off the water at night washes out a lens pointed toward a dock light.

Recording rules worth knowing before you mount anything

New York lets you record video of your own property, but two limits matter. Audio is the tricky part: under the state's eavesdropping law, recording a conversation you are not part of can be a crime, so many installers leave microphones off on cameras covering shared spaces. And New York Labor Law bans video in employee restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas. Aim cameras at your own driveway and doors rather than straight into a neighbor's windows.

In Suffolk, the police department runs the SHIELD camera registry, where residents and businesses can list where their cameras are. Detectives working a case nearby can then ask you to check a date and time; you decide what to share. Registering costs nothing and does not give anyone live access.

What install day looks like

  1. Walkthrough: the crew confirms each camera spot and the job it was assigned, and marks the cable path.
  2. Cable: runs go through the attic, basement or exterior conduit; outdoor entries get sealed and looped.
  3. Mounting: cameras go up on junction boxes or mounts rated for the siding, brick or stucco.
  4. Recorder setup: drive formatted, recording mode set, time synced, default passwords changed.
  5. Aim and test: each view is checked at night with the infrared or color night mode on.
  6. Handover: the phone app on every phone that needs it, plus a short lesson on exporting a clip.

Frequently asked questions

How many CCTV cameras does a Long Island house need?

Most detached houses end up with five to eight once the driveway, side gate, back yard and any detached garage are counted. Assign each spot one job (face, plate or general view) and the number becomes clear.

Can I reuse the old coax from my analog system?

Often, yes. If the coax tests clean, HD-analog cameras on a new DVR reuse it and avoid pulling new cable. Corroded connectors near the water usually just need new ends.

Will my cameras keep recording during a power outage?

Only if the recorder, PoE switch and modem are on a battery backup. A UPS covers short outages and lets the system shut down cleanly in long ones.

Is it legal to record audio on my cameras in New York?

Video of your own property is fine, but recording conversations you are not part of can violate the state's eavesdropping law. Many systems leave audio off on cameras that cover shared or public areas.

Is there a monthly fee for a CCTV system?

No. Footage is stored on a DVR or NVR inside your own building and the phone app reads it from there, so the published Long Island systems carry a $0 monthly cost.
