Long Island apartments come in four shapes

Most of the Island is single-family homes, so its apartment buildings tend to be overlooked. They are there, though, and each type needs a different plan:

  • Village walk-ups and apartments over shops near railroad stations, often older masonry with plaster halls and no drop ceilings.
  • Post-war garden courts, several low buildings around a lawn, usually linked by basements or paths.
  • Elevator co-ops and rentals, including the oceanfront buildings in places like Long Beach, where concrete and salt air set the rules.
  • Newer transit-area rentals with garages, package rooms and corridors that are easy to wire if cable goes in at the right stage of construction.

Step 1: get the approval path right

In a rental, the owner decides, but residents notice new cameras quickly. A short notice explaining which areas are recorded and why reduces complaints. Co-op and condo buildings route common-area camera work through the board, usually via the managing agent. Boards tend to ask the same questions, so bring the answers:

  • A floor-by-floor map showing each camera and what it sees.
  • A sample still from each spot, proving no camera looks into a unit door or window.
  • Who can view live, who can export, and how long footage is kept.
  • Whether audio is recorded (the safe answer for shared areas is no).
  • The installer's certificate of insurance and the hours work will happen.

Step 2: decide what each camera has to prove

A camera that only shows "someone was there" is a weak camera. Choose its job first, then the lens and placement.

Step 4: write the footage policy before go-live

The most common post-install headache is a resident asking for footage of a parking-lot dent. Settle these points in writing before the system goes live:

  1. Retention by area. Longer for entrances, mail and garages; shorter for corridors.
  2. Named exporters. One person at the building and one at the management office.
  3. An export log. Date, camera, time range and who requested the clip.
  4. A request route. Residents, police and insurers all go through the named person, following the owner's or board's lawyer's guidance.
  5. Admin credentials. Held by the owner or board, not only by the installer or a departing super.

Common problems in older Long Island buildings

  • A recorder that quietly stopped writing. Set up health alerts so a failed drive is noticed in a day, not after an incident.
  • A basement switch with no backup power. One outage takes every camera offline; a battery backup fixes it.
  • Entrance cameras blinded by daylight. Cameras facing a glass door need wide dynamic range or a better angle.
  • Coastal corrosion. Near the water, sealed junction boxes and corrosion-resistant mounts are worth the extra cost.

Frequently asked questions

Can a landlord put security cameras in the common areas of a Long Island apartment building?

Generally yes. Lobbies, entrances, hallways, laundry rooms, garages and parking areas are shared spaces where residents have a lower expectation of privacy. Cameras must never look into apartments, bathrooms or other private areas, and many owners post notices that the building is recorded.

Can building cameras record audio?

Usually no. Penal Law §250.05 treats capturing a conversation that no participant agreed to as eavesdropping, and a lobby mic in a Hempstead or Long Beach co-op picks up exactly that. Boards on Long Island almost always order video-only systems.

Does a co-op or condo board have to approve the camera system?

In a co-op or condo the board, or the managing agent acting for it, normally approves anything installed in common areas. Bring a camera map, a sample image from each proposed location and a written footage policy to the meeting; boards approve faster when privacy questions are answered up front.

How much does an apartment building camera system cost on Long Island?

Abstract Enterprises Security Systems lists installed building cameras at $575 to $1,725 per camera in Nassau and $600 to $1,800 in Suffolk. Its packages start from $5,750 (Nassau) or $6,000 (Suffolk) for a four-to-eight-camera village building, and from $13,800 or $14,400 for twelve to twenty cameras.

How long should building footage be kept?

There is no single legal number for a private building. Many boards choose about 30 days for entrances, mail areas and garages, because residents often report a problem days later, and shorter retention for stairs and corridors. Storage is sized to the policy.

Who should be allowed to export footage?

Viewing can be shared with the super and manager, but exporting should be limited to one or two named people, with each export logged. Requests from residents, police or insurers go through that person under the building's written policy.
