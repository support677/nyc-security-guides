Residential Security Camera Installation NYC: Plan Coverage by Home Type, Then Count Cameras

Start with the handful of moments you actually want on video, map where those happen in your type of home, and only then count cameras. Step 1: List the events, not the cameras

Write down what you want to be able to prove or see. For most city households the list is short:

  • Who approached the front door, and what they took or left
  • Who tried a car door in the driveway or at the curb overnight
  • Who came over the back fence or down the side alley
  • Whether a contractor, dog walker or sitter arrived when they said they did

Each item points to a specific spot and a specific detail level. A face at the stoop needs a close, well-lit view; "someone crossed the yard at 3 a.m." needs wide coverage and decent night performance. That is the real spec sheet.

Step 3: Draw the privacy lines first

A question that shows up on r/AskNYC every summer: is a backyard camera even legal in the city? Generally yes, on your own property. Trouble starts when a camera is deliberately aimed to capture a neighbor's private space — into windows or over the fence into their yard. Keep these rules in your plan from the start:

  • Aim at what you own. Use privacy masking in the camera settings to black out a neighbor's windows if they fall into frame.
  • Leave audio off by default. New York lets you record conversations you are part of, but recording other people's conversations without consent can be eavesdropping. Exterior cameras rarely need sound.
  • Never in bedrooms or bathrooms used by others. New York's unlawful-surveillance law treats a camera in a bedroom, bathroom or changing area as presumptively illegitimate. That matters for rented-out rooms and guest spaces.
  • Tell the household. In a two-family or shared home, agree on who gets app access before the cameras go up.

Step 4: Wired, wireless or a mix

Brick and plaster swallow Wi-Fi. A camera that streams fine near the router can stutter at the back of a narrow row house. The practical rule most installers follow here:

  • Wire (PoE over Cat6) the spots that matter most — front door, driveway, rear door. One cable carries power and video, and it records around the clock.
  • Go wireless or battery for awkward add-ons: a shed, a roof hatch, a rental where you cannot drill.
  • Record locally to a recorder (NVR) inside the house if you want to own the footage and skip per-camera cloud fees.

A typical scenario

Picture a Queens two-family with a shared driveway. The owner downstairs wants to catch whoever keeps opening car doors at night; the tenant upstairs mostly wants to see deliveries. A sensible plan: one camera covering the driveway and curb with good night color, one at each entrance at face height, one on the backyard and side path, all wired to a recorder in the basement. The tenant gets app access to the front-door camera only. Five cameras, each with a job, and a clear agreement about who sees what.

FAQ

Is it legal to put security cameras on my house in NYC?

Generally yes, on your own property. Problems arise if a camera is aimed to capture a neighbor's private areas, like their windows or yard, or if it records conversations you are not part of. Aim at what you own, use privacy masking, and leave audio off.

Can I install cameras in my NYC rental apartment?

Inside your own unit, plug-in and battery cameras that don't require drilling usually raise no lease issue. Anything hardwired, drilled into walls or mounted in common areas typically needs landlord or board approval.

How many cameras will my NYC home need?

That's set by your doors and blind spots, not square footage. An apartment often needs one to three, a two-family or row house four to eight, and a detached house or brownstone with a yard six to twelve.

Wired or wireless cameras for a brownstone?

Wired PoE for the front door, rear door and yard, because brick and plaster weaken Wi-Fi and wired cameras record continuously. Wireless works for hard-to-reach add-ons or spots where drilling isn't allowed.

How long will my home cameras keep footage?

Most home systems with a local recorder hold roughly two to four weeks, depending on camera count, resolution and drive size. A storage calculator can estimate your exact number before you buy a drive.

Do home camera systems in NYC need a monthly subscription?

Not if they record to a local NVR inside the home. Cloud-only cameras typically charge per camera each month; a local recorder keeps the footage in your house with no recurring fee.
