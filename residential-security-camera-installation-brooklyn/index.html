Residential Security Camera Installation Brooklyn works best when you plan it the way someone would actually approach your house: from the curb, up the stoop, down the side alley and into the yard. Counting cameras first is how people end up with eight lenses and no clear view of the one door that matters. This guide walks a typical Brooklyn lot front to back, then gives the price bands for each house type. The walk: sidewalk to back fence

Stand on the sidewalk and move toward the back of the property, stopping at each point where someone could reach a door, a window or something worth taking.

  1. Curb and sidewalk. On a detached or semi-detached house in Bay Ridge, Dyker Heights or Marine Park, this is where the car sits. A camera set for plate detail belongs here. On a row-house block with street parking, most owners skip it and keep the lens on their own steps.
  2. Stoop and parlor door. Packages and visitors. A doorbell camera usually covers it; our Brooklyn doorbell guide goes door by door.
  3. Under the stoop. The garden-level door is low, dark and set back. It needs its own camera with real night performance.
  4. Side alley or driveway. Semi-detached and two-family frame houses in Flatbush, Midwood and Canarsie often have a narrow passage to the back. One camera looking down its length catches anyone using it.
  5. Rear yard and garden. Row-house yards back onto other yards, so this is where break-ins tend to start. Full-color night video matters most here because yards are rarely lit.
  6. Cellar door and rear windows. The cellar entrance and any window reachable from a fence or extension.

Most Brooklyn homes end up with four to eight of those points covered. The walk also shows where cable can travel, and on a Brooklyn house that path moves the final number more than how many lenses you buy.

Where the recorder lives and how the cable gets there

In a Brooklyn house the cellar is the natural home for the recorder. It is out of sight, close to the electrical panel and the internet line, and it gives cable a path to the front and back of the house without crossing finished rooms. From there, each camera gets one Cat6 line that carries both power and video (PoE). Wi-Fi cameras look simpler, but a signal that has to cross a brick party wall and a masonry rear wall to reach the yard is the reason so many back-yard cameras show offline. Wired runs are what keep the yard camera recording at 3 a.m.

On the outside, the clean way to attach to brick is in the mortar joints, with conduit colored to match the wall. That keeps a facade repairable and avoids drilling the face of the brick. Expect the recorder to hold roughly two to four weeks of footage, more with a larger drive. To size storage for your own camera count, try the security camera storage calculator.

Two-family owners: you are also the landlord

Brooklyn has a lot of owner-occupied two- and three-family houses, and that changes the plan. Shared and exterior spaces such as the stoop, vestibule, hallway, cellar and yard can be covered. A tenant's apartment is off limits. Leave sound recording switched off in common areas, because recording conversations you are not part of runs into New York eavesdropping law. Telling tenants where the cameras are and what they cover heads off most complaints, and a camera on the shared cellar door often protects the tenant's storage as much as yours.

Keeping the block on your side

Row houses sit inches apart, and a camera aimed slightly wrong ends up watching a neighbor's yard or windows. Aim at your own doors, gates and yard. Use privacy masks in the recorder settings to black out windows next door that fall inside the frame. On a side street with steady traffic, person-and-vehicle detection plus a motion zone that stops at your property line keeps alerts meaningful.

A Saturday-morning example: a Flatbush two-family

A typical request: an owner on the ground floor of a detached frame two-family wants to know who is using the side alley, and the upstairs tenant keeps losing packages. The plan that usually comes out of the walk is a doorbell at the shared front door aimed at the porch, one camera looking down the alley, one on the rear yard and one inside the cellar entrance, all wired back to a recorder in the cellar. Four cameras, no lens on the upstairs unit, audio off in common areas. That fits the row-house and two-family band above; the exact figure depends on how hard the alley run is.

Frequently asked questions

Can my home cameras see the sidewalk or my neighbor's yard?

Public sidewalks have little expectation of privacy, but aiming a camera into a neighbor's yard or windows causes disputes. Point cameras at your own property and use privacy masks to block out anything next door that falls in the frame.

Do I need Landmarks permission for cameras on a brownstone?

On a designated block, anything fixed to the front facade can need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit, so ask LPC before work starts. Rear-yard cameras and mortar-joint mounting with color-matched conduit keep the visible impact small.

Why do my Wi-Fi cameras keep going offline in the back yard?

The signal has to pass through brick and masonry walls and compete with dozens of neighboring networks. A wired PoE camera run from a recorder in the cellar avoids the problem.

How much does a camera system cost for a Brooklyn two-family house?

The Abstract Enterprises Security Systems page lists $2,000 to $4,500 for a row house or two-family with four to eight cameras, including recorder, cabling and labor, with no monthly fee. The exact price follows a free walk-through.

Can I put cameras inside the apartment I rent out?

No. In a two-family the cameras belong on the shared and outdoor spaces, such as the stoop, hallway, cellar and yard. Keep lenses out of the rented apartment and leave sound off in common areas.

How many days of footage will the recorder keep?

Roughly two to four weeks is typical for a home recorder. More cameras or higher resolution shorten that; a bigger hard drive lengthens it, with nothing billed monthly.
