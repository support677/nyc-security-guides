School Security Camera Installation NYC: A Six-Step Checklist for Administrators Before You Sign

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-11

School Security Camera Installation NYC projects are mostly decided before the first camera is chosen: what the law allows, who is paying, when the building is empty and how long footage must be kept. This guide is written for the people who sign off — heads of school, business managers, principals of charter and parochial schools, daycare directors and facilities staff. Work through it with your counsel and board, then take the answers to an installer such as School Security Camera Installation NYC at Abstract Enterprises Security Systems.

One scoping note: DOE-run public school buildings handle security work through the city's own contracting channels. This checklist is mainly for the independent, charter, religious and early-childhood schools that hire their own contractors.

Step 1: Draw the placement map before the quote

New York draws its line at the reasonable expectation of privacy. For city public schools, Section 528 of the NYC Charter directs cameras at entrance and exit doors and permits them in other parts of the grounds as long as people there would not expect privacy. Nonpublic schools are a useful benchmark from that standard, too.

Put the map in writing, camera by camera, and attach it to the contract. It protects the school if a parent or staff member questions a camera later.

Step 2: Rule out facial recognition in writing

In September 2023 the New York State Education Department issued a determination prohibiting schools in the state from buying or using facial recognition technology, and State Technology Law §106-b defines the biometric tools involved. Conventional recording and ordinary analytics — motion zones, line-crossing, loitering alerts — are still available. The risk is cloud platforms that ship face-matching or watchlist features switched on. Add a contract line stating that no facial recognition feature will be enabled, and ask the vendor to show you where it is turned off.

Step 3: Line up the money before the specs

  • Nonpublic schools: NYSED's Nonpublic School Safety Equipment (NPSE) grant reimburses eligible religious and independent schools for certain safety expenses, and published grant summaries list security systems, including cameras and their recording devices, among eligible purchases. Eligibility is pre-determined from NYSED data, so check your school's status and the current year's rules before you sign.
  • Public districts: the Smart Schools Bond Act has funded security technology, and NYSED's 2023 determination applies to it as well.
  • Federal grants: if federal money touches the project, specify NDAA Section 889-compliant hardware from the start. Swapping brands after award is slow and expensive.

Ask for an itemized proposal — cameras, cabling, recorder, storage, licenses, labor — so a grant administrator can map each line to an eligible category.

Step 4: Set a realistic budget

For commercial-grade IP cameras in NYC school buildings, AESS plans on $450 to $950 per camera installed, with the recorder, network switch and storage priced as system-level items. On its published scale, a six-to-eight-camera daycare starts near $6,000, and a big campus spread over several floors can pass $70,000. What moves a school toward the top of that range:

  • Masonry and plaster walls where cable can't ride an open drop ceiling.
  • Lifts for gym and exterior mounts.
  • Retention: 60 or 90 days of storage costs more disk than 30.
  • Cloud platforms with per-camera monthly licenses. Multiply the fee by camera count and five years before comparing against an on-site recorder.

Step 5: Decide retention and who can view

Footage tied to an identifiable student — an injury, a fight, a disciplinary case — can become part of an education record, which brings FERPA into who may see it. If video is stored on a vendor's cloud, New York's Education Law 2-d data-privacy contract requirements are a question for your counsel. A defensible pattern:

  • Keep 30 to 90 days, sized to your insurer's and counsel's advice (NYC DOE uses a 30-day floor in its buildings).
  • Give viewing rights by role: the front desk sees live entry cameras; administrators can search and export.
  • Copy any incident clip to locked-down storage before the recorder overwrites it.
  • Write the policy down before the system goes live.

Step 6: Schedule around the school calendar

Cable pulls through occupied corridors are noisy, and ladders don't belong in a hallway at dismissal. Most schools book the heavy work for summer, winter or spring break, and finish terminations and camera aiming on evenings or weekends. Tell the installer about fire-drill schedules, after-school programs that keep the building occupied, and who holds keys to the closets and roof.

Questions to put in your RFP

  1. Is every camera and recorder NDAA Section 889-compliant, by model number?
  2. Will you confirm in writing that no facial recognition feature will be enabled?
  3. How many days of retention does the proposed storage deliver at our recording settings?
  4. Does the price include new Cat6 to every camera, or reuse of existing cable?
  5. What happens if the recorder fails during the school year — response time and loaner?
  6. Can the camera system share cabling with planned access control or a visitor intercom?
  7. Will the proposal be itemized for grant reimbursement?

A typical small-school scenario

A two-building religious school in the outer boroughs has an old analog recorder, eight cameras of mixed age and no camera at the side door the kids actually use. The sensible plan keeps whatever coax is still sound for interim use, adds IP cameras on new cable at both entrances and the yard, puts a right-sized recorder in a locked closet on battery backup, and writes a one-page viewing policy. The NPSE paperwork is prepared alongside, not after.

Frequently asked questions

Can New York schools use facial recognition cameras?

No. NYSED's September 2023 determination prohibits schools in New York State from purchasing or using facial recognition technology. Standard cameras, recording and common analytics such as motion and line-crossing alerts remain allowed, so confirm any cloud platform has face-matching disabled.

Can a school put cameras in classrooms or bathrooms?

Bathrooms, locker rooms and changing areas are off limits. Classrooms are a policy decision for the administration and counsel, not a default. Entrances, corridors, stairwells, cafeterias, gyms, yards and perimeters are standard camera locations.

How much does a school security camera system cost in NYC?

AESS budgets $450 to $950 for each commercial IP camera, installed, before the recorder, switch and storage; whole projects start near $6,000 for a small daycare and can pass $70,000 on a large campus. Walls, lifts, retention and licensing move the number.

Can private, religious or charter schools get state money for cameras?

Eligible religious and independent schools can seek reimbursement through NYSED's Nonpublic School Safety Equipment (NPSE) grant, whose listed eligible items include security systems with cameras and recording devices. Check your school's eligibility and the current year's rules with NYSED.

How many days of video should a school keep?

Many schools size storage for 30 to 90 days, guided by their insurer and counsel; NYC DOE keeps a 30-day minimum in its buildings. Export any incident clip to secured storage before it is overwritten.

When is the best time to install cameras in a school?

School breaks, especially summer, for cable pulls and drilling, with finishing work on evenings or weekends. Share the calendar, after-school program hours and key holders with the installer early.
