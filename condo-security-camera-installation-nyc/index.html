Condo security camera installation NYC owners agree to fund usually follows one specific event: a parcel gone from the mailroom, a scraped car on the garage ramp, a stranger who slipped in behind a resident. The quickest route to a sound plan is to replay that event and ask what video would have settled it. This guide walks the building in that order, then turns to the system the sponsor left behind, then to the budget. Common elements belong to the board's plan; units do not

Each owner in a condominium has title to an apartment; the lobby, corridors, elevators, garage and amenity floors are common elements that the board manages as the declaration and bylaws direct. That is why the board, not individual owners, picks camera locations and controls the recordings. It also sets firm limits: nothing in restrooms or changing rooms, no audio, and corridor cameras aimed down the hall so an open door never reveals the inside of an apartment. A few buildings' bylaws put large improvements to an owner vote once they pass a dollar threshold, so check yours before anyone signs.

Replay the incident: what each camera has to answer

PlaceQuestion the footage must answerCamera choice that answers it
Sidewalk and front doorWho approached, and did somebody hold the door?A face-height view outside or in the vestibule, plus a wider lobby view
Concierge deskWhat was handed over, and to whom?A fixed, well-lit view over the desk rather than a distorted fisheye
Mailroom or package roomWho carried off which box?One camera over the shelves and one on the door, so the face and the parcel both land on video
Elevator cab and landingsWho rode to which floor, and when?A compact vandal-resistant dome in each cab
Garage and rampWhich vehicle, at what time?Higher-resolution cameras where staff will zoom into recordings, with a dedicated ramp view
Roof door, stairs, service entranceWas a door propped or forced?Small cameras on the doors themselves
Gym, pool, roof deck, bike roomWho was in the space after hours?Cover the entries, not people exercising

One point boards often miss: placement beats megapixels. A 4-megapixel turret set at the right height near a door will pick out faces more reliably than a higher-resolution camera bolted to a lobby ceiling twenty feet up. Spend on 4K where it pays, in garages and plazas where someone will zoom in after the fact.

The sponsor's system: repair, upgrade or replace?

Newer condos often inherit cameras the developer bought, sometimes with the admin password still held by the sponsor's vendor. Before spending a dollar, get every login transferred to the condominium. Then sort the system into one of these rows.

What the survey findsUsually the right move
Most cameras fine, a few dead, recorder healthyRepair: swap failed cameras and drives, update firmware
Cable tests sound, picture soft or still analogUpgrade: new cameras and recorder on the cable that passes
Recorder discontinued, no parts, cable damaged or undocumentedReplace, phased across budget years if needed
Nobody can log in at allRecover or reset access first; nothing else can be judged until then

Retention is the number that sets your drive size

The board decides how many days of video to keep, and storage follows that decision. A lobby camera recording around the clock through constant foot traffic eats storage far quicker than a roof-door camera that only wakes on motion, so the same 30 days needs very different capacity camera by camera. Ask for two or three options, such as 30 and 60 days, each with the drive size behind it. To sanity-check any proposal, run your own camera count through the free security camera storage calculator.

When owners mount their own cameras

A recurring r/AskNYC question asks whether a resident may aim a personal camera at a condo lobby. Commenters often point out that people have little expectation of privacy in shared spaces, but legality is a different matter from the building's rules. Condo-law commentators note that an owner rarely has the right to fix a device to the common elements without board approval. The practical answer is a house rule: doorbell cameras allowed with audio off and aimed at the owner's own doorway, nothing attached to common walls without written permission.

A typical sequence: a 60-unit conversion condo in Brooklyn discovers the sponsor's recorder is out of support and three garage cameras are dark. The board recovers the logins, keeps the garage cable that tests well, and splits the work into two phases, front door and mailroom first, garage the following spring.

Frequently asked questions

Does a condo board need every owner’s consent to install common-area cameras?

Generally no. The board manages the common elements under the declaration and bylaws. Certain bylaws send improvements over a dollar threshold to an owner vote, so check yours and ask counsel about a large project. Telling owners before the install heads off most complaints.

Who can view condo camera footage?

Whoever the board’s policy names, usually the managing agent, the resident manager and perhaps one officer, each with a personal login. Owners do not get automatic access; requests go through the agent, and legal requests through counsel.

Can cameras go on a landmarked condo facade?

Often, if they are small, few and kept off the primary facade where another spot works. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s permit guidebook covers security cameras, so plan exterior views with its criteria in mind.

Should our condo keep the sponsor’s camera system?

Keep whatever tests well. If the cable is sound, new cameras and a recorder can often reuse it. If the recorder is discontinued and nobody holds the admin login, replacing it usually costs less over time than nursing it along.
