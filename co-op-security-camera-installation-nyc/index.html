Co-op security camera installation NYC boards approve tends to go wrong in one predictable place: the cameras arrive before anyone has agreed what they are for. A cooperative is a single corporation with dozens or hundreds of shareholders who all pay through maintenance, so a recorder full of lobby video is a governance question as much as a technical one. Who may look, for how long, and on whose say-so? This guide puts the paperwork first, the hardware second and the money third. Five decisions to put on paper before anyone climbs a ladder

  1. The problem list. Write down real incidents: parcels missing from the vestibule, a laundry coin box forced, bikes gone from the cellar. Each one becomes a camera location with a job, which keeps the plan from drifting into "a camera everywhere."
  2. Spaces in and out of scope. Corporate common areas can be covered. Restrooms, staff changing areas and any view through an apartment door cannot. Record the excluded list in the minutes so it does not get argued again next year.
  3. Who holds the keys. Name the roles, such as managing agent, super and perhaps one officer, that get live view, and separately who gets playback. One Manhattan shareholder on Reddit described a board member watching the feeds nonstop; a written access rule is how a board heads that off.
  4. Retention in days. Choose a number. Property manager FirstService Residential, writing in 2024 about New York condos and co-ops, put typical retention anywhere from 30 to 90 days, scaled to the size of the building. The drives are then sized to your number, not the reverse.
  5. How a clip leaves the building. Requests from shareholders, insurers or police go to the agent in writing and follow counsel's advice. The super should know how to save a clip before the recorder overwrites it.

A one-page camera policy a board can adapt

Few co-ops need a long document. A short policy that answers those five questions, adopted at a properly noticed meeting, is easier to defend than an unwritten habit. New York courts generally defer to co-op boards that act in good faith and within their authority, the business judgment rule set out in Levandusky v. One Fifth Avenue (1990). Have the co-op's attorney check the wording against the proprietary lease and house rules.

Fit the plan to the building you actually have

New York co-ops come in very different shapes, and the hard part of each installation sits somewhere different.

Small walk-upFront door, mailboxes, rear yard or cellar doorFew cameras, but the recorder needs a locked spot that is not a shareholder's closet

Postwar towerLobby desk, garage ramp, loading dock, every elevatorCamera counts climb fast; rack space, a larger recorder and battery backup

On wiring, network cable that also powers each camera from a PoE switch is the normal choice now, so most locations need no new outlet. Where an old analog system left decent coax behind, a hybrid recorder can sometimes reuse it for a while. Battery Wi-Fi cameras are a poor match for masonry: thick walls swallow the signal and somebody ends up on a ladder recharging them.

Reading a camera bid like the co-op's treasurer

Before the vote, confirm each bid answers these in writing:

  • A camera map, with off-limits spaces marked, that matches the policy.
  • Retention in days and the drive capacity that delivers it.
  • Who owns the admin login: the corporation, not the installer or a director who may step down.
  • How the recorder connects to the building network without being exposed to the open internet.
  • Battery backup on the recorder and switch, since old buildings see plenty of power blips.
  • The installer's New York State security license, and an insurance certificate naming the corporation and the agent.
  • Working hours that respect the house rules, and who arranges elevator padding and access.

Here is how it tends to play out. A 50-apartment prewar co-op in Queens has parcels disappearing from a vestibule shelf and coin boxes forced in the laundry room. The security committee lists three problems, the board adopts a one-page policy with 30-day retention, and phase one covers the street door, vestibule, package shelf and laundry. The cellar corridor and rear yard wait for next year's budget, already drawn on the same camera map.

Frequently asked questions

Can a co-op board member watch the building cameras whenever they want?

Only if the board’s policy allows it. Nothing technical prevents it unless accounts are set up by role. A policy that limits live view to the agent and super, with playback only for a logged reason, settles the question, and separate logins show who looked.

Is a shareholder entitled to a copy of lobby footage?

Not automatically. The recordings belong to the corporation. Most policies route requests through the managing agent in writing, and disputes between shareholders go to counsel before anything is released. Ask quickly, because the recorder overwrites after the retention period.

Can a co-op put a camera in the community room shareholders book for parties?

Many boards do, with a clear notice at the door, video only and no audio. CooperatorNews has answered this exact question; the safer setup is visible signage and a rule that footage is reviewed only after a reported incident.

Can our board limit video doorbells on apartment doors?

Boards generally regulate them through house rules, for example requiring audio off and a view of the owner’s own doormat rather than a neighbor’s door. Ask the co-op’s attorney how your proprietary lease handles adopting new house rules.

How long should a co-op keep camera recordings?

Pick a fixed number and write it into the policy. Guidance for New York buildings commonly lands between 30 and 90 days, with larger buildings leaning longer. The drives are then sized so the recorder holds that much before it overwrites.
