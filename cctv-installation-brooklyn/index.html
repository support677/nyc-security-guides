CCTV Installation Brooklyn plans look different on almost every block, because the borough mixes so many kinds of buildings within a few streets: brownstones with stoops and deep rear yards, wood-frame two-families with driveways, brick row houses with a rented basement, glassy new condos, and avenue stores with a stockroom downstairs. The camera count matters less than where the cable can travel and who has to say yes before anyone drills. This guide walks through five common Brooklyn building types, three local checks that people skip, and what an install day looks like. Camera plans for five Brooklyn building types

Brownstone or townhouse (Park Slope, Bed-Stuy, Clinton Hill)

The usual weak points are the stoop and the under-stoop door, the parlor windows on the street side, and the rear yard, which is often reached over a back fence from the next lot. A typical plan puts one camera above the main entry looking down the stoop, one covering the garden-level door, and one or two on the rear wall facing the yard. Cable drops through the parlor floor or rides along the cellar ceiling, and the recorder sits on the garden level or in a closet upstairs.

Frame one- or two-family (Flatbush, Midwood, Marine Park)

These houses tend to have a driveway along one side, a detached garage and an open, unfinished cellar. That makes wiring easy: runs travel along exposed joists and come up through closets or the siding. The driveway and the garage door usually get their own cameras, since cars and bikes are what goes missing.

Brick row house with a basement apartment (Bensonhurst, Bay Ridge, Dyker Heights)

The tricky part is the tenant downstairs. New cable has to avoid the basement unit's finished ceiling, so it often goes up the outside along a downspout or through a closet stack. Owners commonly want the front gate, the side alley and the back door, plus a shared hallway camera.

New condo or converted loft (Downtown Brooklyn, Williamsburg, Gowanus)

Newer construction with hollow walls, open ceiling space and a wiring closet per floor is the easiest place in the borough to pull cable. The work is mostly paperwork: the board or managing agent usually wants a certificate of insurance and an approved scope before anyone touches a common area.

Avenue storefront with a basement (Fulton Street, Flatbush Avenue, 86th Street)

The register, the front door at face height and the basement stairs come first, because deliveries and stock live downstairs. Cable stays above the drop ceiling, and the recorder goes somewhere a customer never walks.

What happens on install day

  1. Walk the plan. The crew lead walks each camera spot with you and confirms the view on a phone before mounting anything.
  2. Test what's already there. Any old coax from a previous system gets tested; clean runs are reused and bad ones are replaced.
  3. Run the cable. New runs go along the cellar, through closets or up the exterior in a tidy path, and wall penetrations are sealed.
  4. Mount and aim. Cameras go up and are aimed at faces and doors, not at the sky or the neighbor's windows.
  5. Set up the recorder. Recording modes, motion zones, the clock and the drive are configured, and the box is connected to your router.
  6. Phone handover. The app goes on your phone, and on a tenant's or manager's phone if you want, limited to the cameras each person needs.

What it costs, and what longer storage buys

The published Brooklyn prices start at $850 for a four-camera recorder system and go to $1,292 for the 4K lines. A four- to eight-camera home system for a brownstone, row house or two-family is listed at $950 to $2,500, and commercial systems at $1,500 to $5,000 and up. Each price bundles the cameras, the recorder, wiring, mounting and the app on your phone, along with roughly fourteen days of footage, and nothing is billed monthly. Small retailers: check the state security credit

Shop owners on the avenues sometimes miss a state program, the Commercial Security Credit: a refundable New York tax credit aimed at small retailers who pay for theft prevention, with cameras on the list of eligible spending. The state tax department sets it at $3,000 per qualifying store. To claim it, you first apply to DCJS, the state criminal justice services agency, for a certificate, and the minimum spend depends on your headcount. Allocation years change, so ask your accountant before you count on it. Either way, keep an itemized invoice that lists the camera system on its own line.

A typical Brooklyn job

An owner of a brick two-family in Flatbush has four old analog cameras and a recorder that died last winter. She lives upstairs and rents the garden floor. Three of the four coax runs test clean and stay. The fourth, which fed a rear-yard camera, is chewed near the fence, so a new Cat6 run goes along the cellar joists to a new camera on the back wall. The recorder moves off the cellar floor onto a wall shelf beside the router, with a battery backup under it. The tenant gets app access to the front door and cellar door views only.

Frequently asked questions

How much does CCTV installation cost in Brooklyn?

The published Brooklyn tiers start at $850 for a four-camera recorder system and reach $1,292 for the 4K lines. A four- to eight-camera home system on a network recorder is listed at $950 to $2,500, and commercial systems at $1,500 to $5,000 and up, and each one comes with the recorder, wiring, mounting, app setup and about fourteen days of footage.

Can I keep the old coax cable from my previous camera system?

Often, yes. If a run tests clean end to end, a modern HD-over-coax camera can use it. Runs with cut ends, corroded connectors or hidden splices are usually replaced with Cat6.

Can my landlord put cameras in the hallway of my Brooklyn building?

Generally an owner may record shared areas and the outside of the building, and listing it in the lease avoids disputes. Cameras inside apartments, and hidden cameras in bathrooms or bedrooms, are not allowed. Recording conversations raises separate consent rules, so many owners keep audio off.

Do CCTV systems have monthly fees?

Not with a local recorder. Footage is stored on the drive at the property and viewed through a free phone app, so there is no monthly charge unless you choose a cloud service.

How long does a CCTV system keep footage?

A standard four-camera system includes about two weeks of continuous recording. Longer storage is a one-time drive upgrade, published at $180 for one month up to $450 for four months.
