School Security Camera Installation Hudson Valley is a different job from wiring a city school, because on a valley campus the toughest coverage problem is rarely the lobby. It is the gym that sits in its own building, the bus loop at the bottom of a hill, the maintenance barn at the end of a quarter-mile drive and the parking lot that has no streetlight. This guide walks a principal, business official or board member through the three questions that set the price and the design: what kind of campus you run, where the money is coming from, and how far apart your buildings are. Start by naming your campus type

The Hudson Valley has every kind of school in a short drive: a home-based daycare in a Rockland split-level, a parochial school in a brick village building with a 1950s block addition, a yeshiva expanding wing by wing, a boarding school spread over acres in Dutchess or Putnam. Each needs a different count of cameras and a different backbone, so the first honest number comes from the type, not from a per-camera average pulled off a national website.

Distance between buildings is the line item nobody budgets

A single city building puts every camera within a few hundred feet of one network closet. A valley campus often does not. Ordinary Ethernet is limited to about 100 meters (328 feet) per run, so a field house across the parking lot, a modular classroom behind the main building or a gate camera at the bottom of the drive needs a different link. The usual options:

  • Fiber between buildings – the most durable answer where a trench or an existing conduit is available, and immune to lightning carried along copper.
  • Point-to-point wireless – a pair of radios with clear line of sight, used where trenching would mean cutting through rock, a paved lot or a wetland setback.
  • Extended-reach PoE or a small outdoor switch – for a camera just past the 100-meter mark on a pole or a fence line.

Ask every bidder to draw these links on the site plan. Two quotes with the same camera count can differ by thousands of dollars purely because one includes a fiber run to the gym and the other quietly assumed Wi-Fi.

Match the hardware to the money

Where the funding comes from changes what can be bought and how the paperwork is kept, so settle it before choosing cameras.

  • Public districts and the Smart Schools Bond Act. The state bond act lists video surveillance among eligible high-tech security projects, and districts publish Smart Schools Investment Plans before spending. New York also bars facial recognition in schools, so a plan that includes cameras should state that the feature is not used.
  • Nonpublic schools and the NPSE grant. The State Education Department's Nonpublic School Safety Equipment program is a reimbursement grant for open religious and independent P-12 schools that hold a valid OSC vendor ID. You spend, document, then claim; the current guidance lists an expense window running through December 31, 2026 and a claim deadline of January 14, 2027, so check the program guidance for the dates that apply to your school.
  • Anything touching federal funds. Section 889 of the federal NDAA prohibits certain manufacturers, including Hikvision and Dahua, on federally funded purchases. Choose a compliant line from the start rather than discovering the problem at claim time.

Winter-proof the outside before you add more cameras

Exterior cameras on a valley campus face single-digit nights, ice on the overhang and low winter sun bouncing off snow in the lot. A short spec list keeps them recording all season:

  1. Housings with a published low-temperature rating and a built-in heater for north-facing and exposed positions.
  2. Wide dynamic range on any camera that looks from a dark vestibule out toward a bright lot.
  3. Infrared sized to real darkness: a field with no streetlight needs more IR reach than a lit city sidewalk.
  4. Sealed cable glands and drip loops so meltwater does not follow the cable into the camera.
  5. Mounts set clear of the roof edge where ice and snow slide off.

A typical valley scenario

Picture a K-8 school on a slope: the main building at the top, a separate gym below, a bus loop between them and a long driveway to the county road. An old analog recorder covers the front door and a hallway, and nothing reaches the gym. A sensible plan keeps any coax that tests clean, adds IP cameras to the entry vestibule, corridors and lot, runs fiber or a wireless bridge to the gym so it lands on the same recorder, puts one camera on the drive entrance and schedules the ceiling work for the February or spring break. The office gets one screen, and two staff members learn to pull and export a clip in under two minutes.

Frequently asked questions

What should a school in the Hudson Valley budget for cameras?

Each commercial IP camera runs $565 to $1,285 installed depending on county, with the recorder and storage priced on top. Published totals are $5,600 to $9,500 for a daycare, $11,400 to $20,000 for a 16-camera village school and $22,800 to $41,000 or more for a 32-camera academy.

Can a nonpublic school get state money for security cameras?

The NPSE grant reimburses eligible safety expenses for open religious and independent P-12 schools that have a valid OSC vendor ID. It is reimbursement-based, so the school pays first and claims afterward; check NYSED program guidance for which equipment and dates qualify.

Are schools in New York allowed to use facial recognition on cameras?

No. New York bars facial recognition technology in schools, and Smart Schools Bond Act plans need an assurance that it is not included. Person and vehicle detection, line-crossing and loitering alerts are still available.

How do we get cameras to a gym or building across the parking lot?

Network cable only runs about 328 feet, so separate buildings usually connect by fiber in a trench or conduit, or by a point-to-point wireless bridge where trenching is impractical. Both let every building record to one system.

How many days of footage should a school keep?

Thirty days is a common minimum because incidents are often reported late; boarding and multi-building campuses often size for 60 to 90 days. Retention is set by drive capacity, so it should be written into the quote.
