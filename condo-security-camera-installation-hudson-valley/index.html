Condo Security Camera Installation Hudson Valley boards are usually protecting a landscape more than a lobby: a townhouse community on a hillside off Route 9, a garden complex with three parking areas and a pool, a 55-plus development with a clubhouse at the end of a long private road, or now and then a downtown condo building in White Plains, Yonkers or Beacon. Distances are long, nights are dark, winters bring snow and ice, and summer storms take out power. A camera that looks fine on a sunny walk-through can be useless at 2 a.m. in a snow squall. This guide is about designing for those conditions, then budgeting and governing the system. Walk the property after dark

Do the planning walk twice: once in daylight to find mounting spots and cable paths, and once after sunset with a board member and a flashlight. At night three things show up that never appear on a site plan. First, the light: a lot lit by two poles is really two pools of light and a lot of black between them. Second, headlights: a camera aimed down the entrance drive will be blinded by every car coming in unless it is angled across the lane instead of straight into it. Third, glare from the clubhouse windows and the pool lighting, which can wash out half the picture. Mark each problem spot on the plan. That is where you spend on better low-light cameras or ask the association's electrician about a fixture.

Snow, rain and the infrared problem

Most outdoor cameras switch to infrared at night, and infrared reflects off falling snow and heavy rain. In a squall the picture fills with bright streaks and the motion alerts never stop. The cure is placement and lighting, not a more expensive camera: keep the built-in illuminator away from the lens's line of sight, mount under an eave or hood where the area right in front of the lens stays dry, and where a spot matters, light it so the camera can stay in color mode. Ice dams and plow piles matter too. A camera mounted low beside a drive gets buried or knocked crooked by a plow by February, so mount entrance cameras high on a pole or building corner, out of the plow's reach.

Count the points before you count cameras

Rather than asking how many cameras the community needs, list the places where something has actually happened or where everyone passes. For most Hudson Valley associations that list looks like this:

  • The main entrance, and any second way in that residents use as a shortcut
  • The cluster mailbox kiosk and any package shelf in the clubhouse
  • The clubhouse doors and the pool gate
  • The trash and recycling enclosure, where dumping disputes start
  • Guest parking and any RV or boat storage area
  • Garage rows that sit away from the homes they serve

Each point gets one job: show who came and went, show what happened in an area, or read a plate at a choke point. A camera asked to do all three does none of them well.

Starting prices by county

The published starting system is four house-brand cameras with a recorder, installed: $1,099 is the Westchester and Rockland figure, Orange and Putnam communities start at $1,199, and Dutchess or Ulster properties at $1,299. The equipment is identical; the difference is travel. Each camera beyond the first four is priced once the property walk confirms the layout. Runs longer than the included length, trenching or boring under drives, new poles and fishing cable through finished clubhouse rooms are each listed separately, and old coax that may not test clean is flagged with a replacement price before anything is ordered. If the association has already bought cameras, the installation can be quoted as labor only from the models and a marked-up aerial photo.

Put surge protection on every run that leaves a building, and put the recorder and network gear on battery backup sized to ride through a real outage, not a flicker.

How long to keep video, and how much drive that takes

Hudson Valley owners travel, and a scraped car in the guest lot may not be noticed for weeks. Thirty days is a sensible minimum for most associations; some boards choose more. Retention is what drives the size and cost of the recorder's drives, so settle the number before the quote. The storage calculator turns a camera count, resolution and retention period into drive space.

Owners' own cameras and the townhouse front

In many townhouse condos the exterior walls, the front stoop and even the door frame are common elements under the declaration, so a doorbell camera or a driveway camera added by an owner is a change to association property. Boards should write a short rule before the first request arrives: what devices are allowed, where they may mount, that audio stays off, and that no camera may look into a neighbor's windows or yard. Condominiums in New York are governed by Article 9-B of the Real Property Law and by their own declaration and bylaws; the association's attorney should review the rule.

Questions to ask before the board votes

  • Can you show us a night sample frame for the entrance and the main lot?
  • What happens to the system during an eight-hour outage?
  • Which runs need digging, and what is the plan if you hit rock?
  • Whose account owns the recorder, and who can export footage?
  • What will it cost to add the next phase of cameras next year?

Frequently asked questions

Why do our cameras show nothing but white streaks when it snows at night?

Infrared light from the camera bounces off falling snow and rain. Moving the illuminator away from the lens, mounting under an eave or hood, and lighting key spots so the camera can stay in color mode fixes most of it.

What does a condo camera system cost in the Hudson Valley?

It depends on the county and the size of the property. A recorder with four cameras, installed, is listed from $1,099 in the two southern counties and up to $1,299 in Dutchess or Ulster. Most associations need more than four, and the added cameras, long runs, trenching and poles are priced after the walk.

How long should a condo association keep camera footage?

Thirty days is a common minimum, and longer suits communities where owners travel and may not notice damage for weeks. The board sets the period and the drives are sized to it.

Can a townhouse owner add their own doorbell or driveway camera?

Often the wall and stoop are common elements, so it usually needs board approval under the declaration and bylaws. A clear written rule on allowed devices, mounting, audio and viewing angles avoids disputes.

Can we reach a pool house or entrance sign without digging?

Often, yes. A wireless point-to-point bridge works where there is a clear line of sight, and fiber in conduit is used where a trench is practical. Long copper runs between buildings are best avoided because of lightning.
