Buying a camera versus buying coverage

Some buyers need a part: a replacement turret for a deli ceiling, a bigger hard drive, a PoE switch for a recorder that has run out of ports. Others need an outcome: being able to read a face at the street door at 2 a.m. and hand a clean clip to a detective. Parts are easy to buy when you already know the model of your recorder and the kind of cable in the walls. Outcomes are harder, because they depend on mounting height, light, lens and storage all working together.

Megapixels are left off that list on purpose. More pixels help when the camera sits far from what it watches; on a short hallway they mostly make bigger files. A sensible mix is a higher-resolution camera on the door, register or driveway and a 2K model everywhere else.

The window problem

A question that keeps coming up on NYC forums is whether a camera can sit inside the apartment and look out through the glass at a stoop, a parked car or a fire escape. In daylight it often works. At night the camera flips to infrared, the IR LEDs reflect off the pane, and the picture turns into white glare with your own room mirrored in it. The workarounds are to switch IR off and rely on street light, to press a camera with a rubber hood flat against the glass, or to mount it outside with the owner's permission. If the view out the window is the reason you are buying, test it after dark before the return period ends.

Wi-Fi, brick and the floor plan

Plaster on metal lath, brick party walls and concrete floors all soak up Wi-Fi. A battery camera that streams fine in the living room may drop out by the back bedroom, and a camera at the street door of a walk-up rarely reaches a router three flights up. Before buying several Wi-Fi cameras, stand where each one will go and run a speed test on your phone; if the upload is weak there, the camera will struggle too. Where a cable can be run, a wired PoE camera removes the guesswork: one Ethernet line carries power and video, and the footage lands on a recorder you control rather than a cloud plan.

Size the recorder before the cameras

People usually pick cameras first and storage last, then discover the drive overwrites itself after a long weekend. Work in the other direction:

  1. Decide how many days you need to keep. Stores often want at least two weeks, because a theft is frequently noticed days after it happened.
  2. Count the cameras and leave room to grow: an 8-channel recorder holding seven cameras still has space for one more.
  3. Choose resolution and codec per camera. H.265 can cut storage substantially compared with H.264 at similar picture quality.
  4. Pick a surveillance-rated drive from those numbers, and put the recorder somewhere dry, ventilated and locked, on a small battery backup so a power blip does not corrupt it.

Permission comes before mounting

A camera inside your own apartment is generally your own business. Anything in a common hallway, on the facade or aimed from a fire escape usually needs the landlord, managing agent or co-op or condo board to agree, and a landmarked building adds limits on what may be fixed to the front. Recording sound raises separate eavesdropping questions under New York law, so leave microphones off in shared spaces unless you have checked.

A shopping checklist

  • Is there already a recorder in the building, and is it analog HD or IP? The new camera has to match it.
  • What exactly must the camera identify, and from how many feet?
  • Does the camera face a bright window or doorway?
  • Who has to approve the mounting spot?
  • How many days must footage last, and who knows how to export it?

A short scenario

A laundromat owner in Queens calls asking for "two more cameras." The existing DVR is analog HD, the folding tables sit against a sunny glass front, and the drive holds five days. Two random cameras would not fix that. What does: one WDR camera turned to face away from the glass, a replacement drive large enough for two weeks, and analog HD cameras that match the recorder already there instead of IP models that would force a new NVR.

Frequently asked questions

Is a security camera worth it for an NYC apartment?

For many renters, yes: one or two indoor cameras show who came in and when, and let you check on pets or deliveries. Pick one that records locally or has clear privacy controls, and keep it pointed inside your own unit rather than at neighbors' doors.

Can I put a camera on my apartment door?

Peephole cameras and video doorbells that fit on or in the door are common. The hallway is a common area, so check your lease, co-op house rules or with management before installing one.

Can I ask my landlord to add cameras in the building?

Yes. Put the request to the landlord or managing agent in writing and explain the problem, such as package theft or break-ins. Mounting your own cameras in common areas without permission can start a lease dispute.

Do security cameras need a monthly subscription?

Not with a recorder-based system. Footage stays on a local hard drive and phone viewing runs through the recorder's app. Many Wi-Fi camera brands charge a monthly fee to keep cloud history.
