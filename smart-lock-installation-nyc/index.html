Smart Lock Installation NYC: Who Controls the Door Decides Which Lock Goes On It

By Abstract Enterprises Security Systems — licensed NYS low-voltage contractor, in security since 2000 · Updated 2026-11-11

Smart lock installation NYC projects seldom go wrong because of the hardware itself. They fail because nobody settled who controls the door before the box was opened. A renter, a co-op shareholder, a two-family owner and a deli owner can all buy the same keypad deadbolt, and each one faces a different set of rules about what may come off the door, who must keep a key and what the inside of the door has to do in a fire. This guide sorts those four situations first, then matches the lock to the door. Step two: look at what is physically on the door

Take two photos before you shop: the inside of the door and the edge where the bolt comes out. Those two pictures answer most compatibility questions.

A standalone deadbolt you turn by hand indoors

This is the easy case. Most consumer smart locks and every retrofit unit are designed for a round, bored, single-cylinder deadbolt. Renters on Reddit's NYC and smart-home boards report fitting retrofit kits with no permanent changes; the outside cylinder and the landlord's key stay as they were.

A mortise lockset: lever handle plus keyed cylinder

Common on heavy pre-war doors from Jackson Heights to Washington Heights. The lock body sits in a rectangular pocket cut into the door edge, so a standard smart deadbolt will not drop in. Options are a smart lock built in mortise format, a trim-and-cylinder conversion where a product supports it, or a separate added deadbolt if the door has room. Measure the door thickness and backset first; old doors are often thicker than a consumer lock's tailpiece allows.

A steel-clad or fire-rated door

Many older apartment and stair doors are rated assemblies. New holes on one of these is a decision to make with the building, not on the spot. Approaches that reuse the existing bore or pocket are the safer route.

Step three: choose a connection that survives the hallway

An apartment entrance is usually the worst spot in the unit for a radio signal: masonry walls, a long corridor and dozens of neighbours' networks.

The real test is simple: switch your phone to mobile data, walk to the street and lock the door remotely. If that fails, the install is not done.

Three situations we hear about constantly

The landlord went keyless overnight. A tenant in a small Queens building comes home to a new app-controlled door and no key. The Tenant Data Privacy Act says building owners with smart entry systems cannot make the system the only way into a tenant's own unit, and must get consent before collecting tenants' data. A keyed override with a physical key for each tenant resolves most of these disputes.

The renter who wants convenience without a fight. A retrofit unit plus a two-line note to management naming the lock and confirming the landlord's key still works. Bag and label the old turn-piece and its screws so the door can be restored when you move.

The owner changing a code at turnover. Deleting a sitting occupant's code is legally the same as changing their lock. Remove access only after the unit has lawfully come back to you, and keep a written log of every code and fob issued and removed.

Questions to ask any installer before they drill

  • Will the inside of the door still open with one motion, no key or code? (Double-cylinder locks are not allowed on NYC apartment doors.)
  • Does the plan keep a mechanical key path for the landlord or board?
  • Have you measured door thickness, backset and the lock pocket, or are you guessing from the box?
  • Will you align the strike so the closer pulls the door shut and latched without a push, so the bolt can throw?
  • Can you provide proof of insurance to my managing agent and work within the building's contractor hours?
  • Where will the bridge or border router live, and will you prove remote access from cellular data?

Frequently asked questions

Can a NYC renter install a smart lock without the landlord's permission?

New York's Multiple Dwelling Law § 51-c lets a tenant in a multiple dwelling add their own lock to the apartment entrance door, no more than three inches in circumference, as long as the landlord gets a duplicate key on request. What it grants is the right to add a lock, not to remove the owner's, so a retrofit over the current deadbolt or a small secondary lock keeps you on safe ground.

Can my landlord make an app the only way into my apartment?

No. Local Law 63 of 2021, known as the Tenant Data Privacy Act, says owners whose buildings use smart entry systems may not make them the only way a tenant can get into their own apartment, and they need express consent before collecting tenants' data. In practice a key or other non-smart way in has to stay available.

Does my co-op or condo board need to approve a smart lock?

That comes down to house rules and what changes. A retrofit that leaves the corridor side untouched rarely needs sign-off; swapping the whole deadbolt, drilling new holes, changing the visible hardware or touching a fire-rated door often does. Most proprietary leases also require the shareholder to give the building a working duplicate key.

Is a keypad lock that needs a code to get out legal on a NYC apartment door?

No. HPD fire-safety guidance says double-cylinder locks, which require a key to get out, are not allowed on apartment doors. Any lock on a dwelling entrance should open from inside with a thumb-turn or lever and no key, code or phone.

Why does my smart lock work at the door but not remotely?

Usually the bridge or the lock's Wi-Fi can't hold a signal at the door, which is common at the end of a masonry or concrete corridor. Moving the bridge to an outlet inside the unit closer to the door, adding a mesh node, or using a Thread lock with a nearby border router normally fixes it.
