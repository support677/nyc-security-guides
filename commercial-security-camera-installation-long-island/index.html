Start with the business, not the camera

Retail and showrooms

The questions that matter are who walked out with what, and what happened at the register. That means a high-resolution camera facing the entrance at face height, a view over each point of sale, coverage of the stockroom and back door, and at least one exterior view of the storefront and parking. Strip centers along Hempstead Turnpike or Sunrise Highway often share a rear service lane with other tenants, so the back-door camera is frequently the one that settles disputes.

Restaurants and food service

Owners usually want to confirm opening and closing routines, the cash drawer, deliveries at the rear and the dining room. Kitchens are hot and greasy, so housings and lenses need to be chosen and placed so they can be wiped down and do not sit directly over the line.

Offices and medical suites

Discreet domes at the lobby, reception and corridor intersections, plus the doors to records rooms or IT closets. Many Route 110 and Garden City offices pair cameras with card access so a door event and its video can be pulled together.

Warehouses, yards and auto lots

Docks, aisles, the yard fence and the gate. Big open spaces are where a pan-tilt-zoom or a multi-sensor camera can do the work of several fixed units, and where plate-reading cameras at the gate earn their cost. Long cable runs and metal buildings are also why these sites should be wired with PoE rather than consumer Wi-Fi cameras.

New York rules a Long Island business has to plan around

  • Telling employees. New York's electronic monitoring law (Civil Rights Law §52-c) says a private employer must hand new hires a written notice about electronic monitoring, collect their acknowledgment, and post it where staff can see it. Plan this before the cameras go live.
  • Audio. Recording conversations you are not a party to can be eavesdropping under Penal Law §250.05. Most commercial systems should be installed with audio off.
  • Private spaces. Cameras in restrooms, changing areas and similar spaces are off-limits under the unlawful surveillance statutes (Penal Law §250.45).
  • Federally funded sites. Section 889 of the 2019 NDAA restricts certain manufacturers on federally funded work, which matters for schools, some clinics and contractors.
  • Village facades. Several Long Island villages have historic districts or architectural review for exterior changes; ask before bolting conduit to a main-street storefront.

These points are background for planning; talk to your attorney about your own situation.

What a clean install day looks like

  1. The crew confirms each camera location against the approved plan with the owner or manager on site.
  2. Cable is pulled from each camera position back to the network closet, through ceilings and walls, with any rated penetrations sealed.
  3. The recorder and PoE switch are mounted, powered and labeled.
  4. Cameras are mounted, aimed and focused while someone watches the live view, checking that faces at the door and the register are readable.
  5. Motion and person detection zones are set so alerts mean something.
  6. Managers get phone access, and the crew shows how to find and export a clip.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a business camera system cost on Long Island?

Abstract Enterprises Security Systems lists $500 to $2,500 per camera installed, with typical ranges of $4,000 to $9,000 for a 4–6 camera retail or office system and $18,000 to $35,000 or more for a 16–30 camera warehouse. The fixed price comes after a site survey.

Do I have to tell employees about security cameras in New York?

Yes. New York Civil Rights Law §52-c says private employers must notify new hires in writing about electronic monitoring, have them acknowledge it and post the notice. Treat this as background, not legal advice.

Can my business cameras record audio?

It is usually best to leave audio off. Under New York Penal Law §250.05, capturing conversations without a participant’s consent can count as eavesdropping.

NVR or cloud for a single Long Island store?

For one location, a local NVR usually costs less over time because there is no per-camera monthly fee. Cloud platforms make more sense when you manage several sites or have no one to look after on-site hardware.

How many cameras does a small business need?

It depends on doors, registers and blind spots rather than square footage. Small retail often starts around 4–6 cameras covering the entrance, register, floor, stockroom and exterior; the survey sets the exact count.
